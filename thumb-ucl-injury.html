Thumb UCL Injury

Ulnar collateral ligament of the metacarpophalangeal joint — "skier's" & "gamekeeper's" thumb

Joint: 1st MCP Ligament: UCL Mechanism: Forced abduction Key lesion: Stener

01Overview

The thumb MCP is one of the most commonly injured joints in the hand, and the UCL is the structure most often torn.

UCL
Most-injured MCP structure
Abduction
Radially-driven force in pinch/grip
Stener
Aponeurosis blocks healing → surgery

The UCL resists radial (valgus) deviation of the thumb during pinch and grasp, so it fails when an abduction/radially-directed load is applied — classically a skier falling onto a planted pole. R&G Ch.45

  • Skier's thumb = the acute injury; gamekeeper's thumb historically the chronic/attritional form.
  • Radial collateral (RCL) injuries and frank dislocations are less common; RCL fails with an ulnar-directed force.
  • If missed, a complete tear leads to chronic instability and weakness of pinch. AO
High-yield Clinical examination remains the decision-making gold standard. MRI tends to underestimate the injury; ultrasound is accurate but operator-dependent. R&G Ch.45
Naming & terminology

"Skier's thumb" describes the acute abduction rupture; "gamekeeper's thumb" was originally coined for chronic attritional laxity from repetitive stress. In current usage the terms are often applied interchangeably, but the acute-versus-chronic distinction matters because chronic tears may be retracted or attenuated and less amenable to direct repair.

02Anatomy

Two ligaments, one aponeurosis — the interplay decides whether a tear can heal on its own.

The UCL complex

  • Proper collateral ligament (pUCL) — runs from the metacarpal head to the base of the proximal phalanx; taut in flexion, the primary restraint to valgus.
  • Accessory collateral ligament — volar to the proper ligament, blends with the volar plate and sesamoid; taut in extension.

Because the two components tighten in different positions, stability must be tested in both flexion and extension (see Physical Exam).

The adductor aponeurosis — why the Stener lesion exists

On the ulnar side of the MCP joint, the transverse and oblique fibers of the adductor pollicis aponeurosis (with some muscle fibers) insert into the EPL tendon and overlie the UCL. AO When the ligament ruptures off the phalangeal base and retracts, its stump can flip dorsal/superficial to this aponeurosis — the aponeurosis then interposes between the torn ends and prevents healing. AO · R&G Ch.45

03Physical Exam

Valgus stress in two positions, read against the uninjured thumb.

  • Tenderness along the ulnar aspect of the MCP joint.
  • Valgus stress testing performed in two positions to isolate each part of the complex.
Stress-test explorer — tap a position
Positivity — indicates a complete tear
  • Greater than 30° of valgus laxity, or
  • Increase of more than 15° compared to the contralateral uninjured thumb,
  • combined with loss of a firm endpoint → complete (Grade III) rupture.
Practical tip Obtain radiographs before stressing to exclude an avulsion fragment off the ulnar phalangeal base — stressing a bony avulsion risks displacing it. Baseline joint laxity varies widely, so always compare to the other thumb. R&G Ch.45
Why the two positions?

In full extension, the volar plate and accessory collateral ligament are the taut structures, so laxity here reflects those. In ~40° of flexion, the volar plate and accessory ligament relax and the proper UCL becomes the primary restraint — laxity in flexion therefore isolates a proper-UCL tear. Testing both avoids missing an isolated proper-ligament injury that looks stable in extension.

04Classification

Rockwood & Green grade ligament injury by laxity and endpoint; the Stener lesion is the surgical subset.

The line that matters Grade I–II (endpoint present) generally heal with immobilization. Grade III with a Stener lesion cannot appose and heal on its own — it is a surgical injury. R&G Ch.45 · AO

Operative repair — how it goes

AO — reattachment options For the displaced ligament, AO describes two techniques: suture anchors or bone tunneling. Avulsion fractures are handled like other proximal phalangeal base fractures. AO
Nonoperative details for a complete tear

Operative repair is standard for a complete tear/Stener lesion because interposed aponeurosis prevents healing. Nonoperative management is reserved for patients whose comorbidities preclude surgery — spica or hinged bracing in slight ulnar deviation and flexion for roughly 4–8 weeks, then graded therapy — with the understanding that persistent instability is common when a true Stener lesion is treated closed.

06Approach

Dorsoulnar approach to the thumb MCP joint. AO

Indications

  • Avulsion of the UCL attachment at the proximal phalangeal base (Stener's / gamekeeper's / skier's thumb).
  • Intra-articular fractures of the ulnar margin of the first metacarpal head.
  • Avulsion fractures of the base of the proximal phalanx.

Key steps & safety

  • Incision starting dorsally ~1 cm proximal to the MCP joint, curving palmarly around the ulnar aspect to ~1 cm distal; oblique or lazy-S shape.
  • Identify and protect divisions of the dorsal sensory branch of the radial nerve with a vessel loop.
  • Divide the adductor aponeurosis to expose the retracted ligament; repair, then close the aponeurosis and capsule.
Watch-out The dorsal sensory branch of the radial nerve runs superficially in the subcutaneous tissue over this region — a neuroma here is a preventable complication. AO

07Complications & Associated Injuries

Most are the price of a missed or under-treated tear.

Associated injuries R&G Ch.45

  • Articular avulsion / shear fractures (the most common association).
  • Capsular disruption.
  • Sesamoid fractures.
  • Adjacent tendon rupture.

Complications

  • Chronic instability & weak pinch — the hallmark of a missed complete tear. AO
  • Persistent instability after closed treatment of a true Stener lesion.
  • Dorsal radial sensory neuroma (surgical).
  • MCP stiffness; late post-traumatic arthritis in chronic/neglected injuries.
Bottom line The whole assessment exists to answer one question: is there a complete tear (± Stener lesion) that needs the OR, or a partial tear that will heal in a splint?
