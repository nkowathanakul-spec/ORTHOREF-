Femoral Neck Fractures

Intracapsular fractures of the proximal femur — assessment, classification, the fix-versus-replace decision, surgical approaches, and complications.

Source: Rockwood & Green’s Fractures in Adults, 10th ed., Ch. 53 · AO Foundation Surgery Reference

Overview & Assessment

Epidemiology

  • Occur most frequently in elderly female patients; ~59% of hip fractures are intracapsular per recent database reports.
  • Lifetime risk of hip fracture: 40–50% in women, 13–22% in men.
  • Risk of a second hip fracture within 2 years approaches 10% (women) / 5% (men); >70% are the same fracture type.
  • Each standard deviation drop in femoral neck bone density raises fracture risk 2.6× (Cummings).
  • Risk factors split into those increasing falls (muscle weakness, gait/balance, neurologic disease, eyesight, sedatives) and those reducing bone mass (osteoporosis, low BMI <18.5, steroids, smoking, alcohol).

Mechanism of Injury

  • 90% are due to falls — usually a simple fall transmitting force through the greater trochanter.
  • Frail patients tend to fall sideways onto the trochanteric region; fitter patients fall forward (more wrist/upper-limb fractures).
  • An alternative mechanism is forced external rotation: the head stays fixed while the neck rotates — explaining the characteristic posterior neck comminution.
  • The fracture occurs at the weakest part of the neck, just below the articular surface (maximal proximal/superolateral bone loss).
  • High-energy trauma (MVA, falls from height) and stress fractures (military recruits; female athlete triad) account for the minority in younger patients.

Signs & Symptoms

Displaced fracture

  • Leg typically shortened and externally rotated.
  • All hip motion painful.
  • Clinically indistinguishable from extracapsular hip fracture.

Undisplaced fracture

  • Findings may be minimal — possibly only a painful range of motion, no deformity.
  • 25–30% of older patients have cognitive impairment → unreliable history.
  • Always consider an acute medical event as the cause of the fall.

Diagnostic Imaging

  • AP and lateral plain radiographs identify most fractures. Diagnosis is usually clear on the AP; the lateral is key to judging displacement and posterior tilt — which drives treatment choice.
  • For a suspected fracture with normal/equivocal radiographs, MRI is the recommended additional modality — more accurate than CT or bone scan, no radiation, and detects soft-tissue causes of pain.
  • CT (sensitivity ~86–100%) is an alternative when MRI is unavailable; bone scan may be falsely negative within 48–72 h in osteopenic bone.

Pathoanatomy & Applied Anatomy

The intracapsular blood supply is the central anatomical fact of this fracture — it explains both AVN and the logic of fix-versus-replace.

Skeletal Anatomy

  • Neck–shaft angle averages 130° (men) / 128° (women). Lower = coxa vara; higher = coxa valga.
  • Femoral neck anteversion is normally 15–25° to the transcondylar axis.
  • The calcar femorale is a dense vertical plate from the posteromedial shaft (below the lesser trochanter) reinforcing the posteroinferior neck.
  • Increased hip axis length, increased neck width, and lower neck–shaft angle all raise fracture risk (longer lever arm, greater force). Hip axis length is longer in White females — partly explaining higher susceptibility.
  • Osteoporosis preferentially removes trabecular bone away from main stress lines (greater trochanter, lateral neck), where bone becomes weakest.

Blood Supply — the Critical Anatomy

  • Three sources: retinacular vessels (most important), intramedullary vessels, and the foveal artery (ligamentum teres).
  • The deep branch of the MFCA provides the dominant supply — ~82% of head perfusion vs ~18% from the LFCA (Dewar cadaveric MRI).
  • The MFCA runs anterior to the conjoined tendon and perforates the capsule just cranial to the superior gemellus and distal to piriformis. Incise the conjoined tendon ≥1.5 cm medial to the trochanteric crest to preserve it.
  • Dividing quadratus femoris off the femoral side in a posterior approach risks the descending MFCA branch.
  • The intracapsular neck has no cambial periosteal layer — union depends on endosteal healing alone, contributing to slow union and nonunion.
  • A displaced subcapital fracture tears the retinacular vessels → risk of AVN if the head is retained. The inferior gluteal artery anastomosis (along the inferior piriformis border) may be compensatory.
Why it matters
The dominance and vulnerability of the MFCA retinacular supply is the whole rationale for replacing (rather than fixing) the displaced subcapital head in older patients, and for the careful posterior dissection technique that spares the artery.

Ligaments

  • The capsule extends to the intertrochanteric line anteriorly, but posteriorly the lateral half of the neck is extracapsular.
  • Iliofemoral ligament (Y of Bigelow) — anterior, strongest; limits hyperextension and external rotation.
  • Pubofemoral — controls external rotation in extension. Ischiofemoral — posterior, weaker; controls internal rotation.
  • Tension in the iliofemoral ligament contributes to fracture causation and posterior neck comminution.

Sensory Supply & Muscles

  • Sensory innervation: obturator (anteromedial), femoral (anterior capsule), sciatic (posterior), superior gluteal (posterolateral). Femoral nerve blocks give incomplete relief (mostly via reduced spasm).
  • Iliopsoas (lesser trochanter) pulls the shaft into external rotation in a fracture; piriformis, gemelli and obturator internus also externally rotate.
  • Abductors (gluteus medius/minimus, superior gluteal nerve) — injury during the direct lateral approach causes Trendelenburg gait.

Classification

The clinically decisive distinctions are intracapsular vs extracapsular and displaced vs undisplaced — these predict complications and drive treatment. Named systems add nuance but show variable reliability.

Bottom line
Surgeons agree well on displaced vs undisplaced (the decision that matters) but poorly on finer subgroups. No newer system has proven superior to these simple groupings for treatment selection.

Treatment & Management

Goals of hip-fracture care are simple: early surgery to reduce complications, early mobility to restore function, and — for those near end of life — surgery as effective palliation. Shared orthopaedic–geriatric care underpins all of it.

Perioperative Principles

  • Timing: early surgery is safe and associated with fewer complications and shorter stay; only a short list of correctable problems justifies delay (e.g. Hb <80 g/L, Na <120 or >150, K <2.8 or >6.0, uncontrolled diabetes, acute LVF, ventricular rate >120).
  • Anaesthesia: large RCTs show no clinically important difference between spinal and general anaesthesia.
  • Blood: expect ~1,000 mL loss / Hb drop of 15–20 g/L. Tranexamic acid for essentially all patients; no role for liberal transfusion.
  • Assess cognition (AMT4), frailty (Clinical Frailty Scale), comorbidities and medications on admission. ~98% of patients proceed to operation in mature systems.

Nonoperative Treatment

  • An option only in undisplaced fractures — but the risk of secondary displacement is high (19–46%), and operative treatment gives better outcomes in comparative studies.
  • Reserved for patients actively dying / terminal illness, the occasional patient declining surgery, and non-ambulant long-term-care residents.

Undisplaced Fractures — Fixation

  • Fixation is the treatment of choice irrespective of age (rare exception: pre-existing symptomatic arthritis → arthroplasty).
  • Implant: cannulated screws (commonly three, inverted triangle) or a sliding hip screw — the FAITH trial showed little meaningful difference (slightly more AVN with SHS, 9% vs 5%).
  • Mobilise weight-bearing as tolerated. Union >90%; nonunion ~7%; late AVN ~5.8%. All-cause reoperation ~14% (FAITH).
  • Posterior tilt ≥20° on the lateral flags higher reoperation risk.

Displaced Fractures — Fix vs Replace

Treatment is stratified by age, function, cognition and comorbidity. Arthroplasty is now the better surgical option for most displaced intracapsular fractures.

Reduction & Fixation Technique Points

  • Gentle traction + internal rotation; avoid forceful manoeuvres (risk of a hard-to-correct valgus reduction).
  • Judge reduction by Lowell’s S-curve (see Overview) and the Garden alignment index (160–180° acceptable on both views).
  • Valgus reduction is preferable to varus — varus >20° carries ~55% failure risk. Anatomic reduction minimises AVN.
  • In young Pauwels III patterns: a fixed-angle device or a medial antiglide (buttress) plate via a Smith-Petersen approach resists shear (failure 29% vs 49% with classical fixation).

Arthroplasty Points

  • Always cement the stem — WHiTE 5 and Cochrane show better HRQoL at 4 months and lower mortality at 12 months vs uncemented; fewer periprosthetic fractures (NNT 26 to keep one extra patient alive at 1 year).
  • Unipolar vs bipolar HA: no clear clinical difference. Bipolar dislocations are harder to reduce closed (risk of head–stem dissociation).
  • THA gives better function than HA in selected fitter patients, at a higher dislocation/infection cost.
  • Approach matters for dislocation: lateral/anterior < posterior (see Approaches tab).

Surgical Approaches

Three workhorse exposures, each with a distinct role: anterior for open reduction and medial plating, lateral for low-dislocation arthroplasty, posterior for extensile arthroplasty access.

Infection

  • Lower after fixation (shorter, minimally invasive, smaller implants) than arthroplasty. Modern deep infection: ~0.18% (fixation), ~1.26% (HA), though some series report 5–6%.
  • Deep arthroplasty infection is a grave problem in frail patients — debridement & retention succeeds in only ~43–44% of HA cases; 1-year mortality 40–50% with prosthetic joint infection.

Fixation Failure & Nonunion

  • Common after displaced-fracture fixation; rare (failure) but ~7% (nonunion) after undisplaced. Slow union increases failure risk.
  • Presents with increasing pain, leg shortening/collapse, radiographic loss of reduction.
  • Usual salvage: conversion to arthroplasty (THA if fit; HA if comorbid with a normal acetabulum) — but salvage carries higher complication rates than primary arthroplasty.

Nonunion in Young Patients

Arthroplasty is not the ideal first salvage under ~40 with no comorbidity. Options: revision of fixation, vascularised/muscle-pedicle bone graft, or valgus osteotomy (if recognised before complete displacement).

Avascular Necrosis

  • Modern incidence ~6.9% (was 16–24% historically) — falling as arthroplasty replaces fixation. Higher in displaced (~27.6% historically) than undisplaced (~16%) fractures; in under-60s, 14.7% (displaced) vs 6.4% (undisplaced).
  • Presents late — most commonly in the second year; younger patients warrant 2-year follow-up with 6-monthly radiographs.
  • Many cases are asymptomatic (~24%) or tolerable (~46%); only a minority need surgery. Symptomatic collapse → usually THA.

Prosthesis Dislocation & Loosening

  • The most common reason for revision within 2 years. HA ~2–3%; THA historically higher (~8–11%, now declining).
  • Approach is a major determinant: switching posterior → direct lateral reduced dislocation from 8%→2% and 13%→4% in two cohorts. Parkinson disease raises risk.
  • Unipolar/THA usually reduce closed; bipolar often needs open reduction (risk of head–stem dissociation).

Thromboembolism

  • Modern DVT risk ~1.8% with early mobilisation. Standard prophylaxis: mechanical + chemical.
  • NICE recommends first-line 28 days of LMWH or fondaparinux (acknowledged low-quality evidence). Aspirin and pneumatic compression are also used.
