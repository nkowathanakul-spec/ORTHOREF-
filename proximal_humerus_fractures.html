Mechanisms of Injury

  • Low energy — Fall from standing height in elderly (osteoporotic bone)
  • High energy — MVA, sports injury in younger patients
  • Indirect — Axial load through outstretched hand (FOOSH)
  • Direct — Blow to lateral shoulder
  • Pathologic — Metastatic disease, Paget's
  • Electrocution / seizure — Posterior fracture-dislocation mechanism

Associated Injuries

  • 45% associated rotator cuff tear (increases with age)
  • Axillary nerve injury — most common neurologic injury
  • Brachial plexus injury in high-energy trauma
  • Axillary artery injury — rare but limb-threatening
  • Pneumothorax / rib fractures in polytrauma
  • Glenohumeral dislocation — fracture-dislocation pattern

02Applied Anatomy

Osseous Landmarks & Vascular Supply

Fig 36-x (R&G 10th Ed). Vascular anatomy of the proximal humerus. The arcuate artery (ramus ascendens of AHCA) is the dominant supply to the humeral head. PHCA provides collateral via posteromedial periosteum.

Four-Part Anatomy (Neer)

  • Humeral head — articular segment, retroversion ~30°
  • Greater tuberosity — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor insertions
  • Lesser tuberosity — subscapularis insertion
  • Shaft — pectoralis major inserts medially (deforming force)
Caution

AVN Risk: Disruption of the arcuate artery + posteromedial periosteal stripping → high risk of humeral head avascular necrosis. Head-splitting and 4-part fractures carry >13% AVN rate (R&G 10th Ed).

Critical Vascular Anatomy

  • Anterior humeral circumflex artery (AHCA) — gives off the arcuate artery (ramus ascendens), main supply to humeral head
  • Arcuate artery — runs in the lateral bicipital groove, enters head at surgical neck; at-risk in fracture and surgical dissection
  • Posterior humeral circumflex artery (PHCA) — provides collateral supply via posteromedial periosteum; preservation critical in surgical neck fractures
  • Medial hinge of posteromedial periosteum is the key to humeral head viability

Muscular Deforming Forces

  • Humeral head — rotator cuff muscle balance; tends to extension/external rotation
  • Shaft — pectoralis major pulls medially and anteriorly
  • Greater tuberosity — supraspinatus/infraspinatus pull superoposteriorly
  • Lesser tuberosity — subscapularis pulls medially

Axillary Nerve

  • Exits quadrilateral space with posterior circumflex vessels
  • Anterior branch wraps around surgical neck at ~6 cm below acromion
  • At risk in deltoid-splitting approaches >5 cm distal to acromion
  • Most common nerve injury in proximal humerus fractures

03Imaging

Radiographic Evaluation

Fig 36-3 (R&G 10th Ed). Radiographic trauma series. A: AP Grashey — tangential view of glenoid articular surface. B: Neer lateral (Y-view). C: Axillary view — confirms humeral head position on glenoid in transverse plane.

Trauma Series — 3 Views

  • AP Grashey — true AP of glenohumeral joint; beam angled 30–45° medially; evaluates tuberosity displacement and head-neck relationship
  • Neer Lateral (Y-view) — scapular Y; assesses anterior/posterior displacement and fracture-dislocation
  • Axillary view — ESSENTIAL; confirms glenohumeral relationship, detects posterior dislocation (often missed on AP alone), evaluates glenoid rim
Clinical Pearl

Velpeau Axillary: If patient cannot abduct arm, obtain Velpeau axillary (patient leans back over cassette) — do not skip axillary view.

CT Indications

  • Complex multipart fractures (3- or 4-part)
  • Suspected fracture-dislocation
  • Head-splitting or impression fractures
  • Preoperative planning for ORIF or arthroplasty
  • 3D reconstruction aids implant templating

CT Protocol Tips

  • 1 mm axial cuts through the shoulder girdle
  • Coronal and sagittal reconstructions for angulation assessment
  • 3D surface rendering for complex comminution and surgical planning
  • Assess glenoid version and bone stock if arthroplasty considered

04Classification

Fracture Classification Systems

Fig 36-12 (R&G 10th Ed). Neer proximal humerus fracture classification. Numbers represent fracture type; percentages indicate incidence; ages are median at presentation.

Neer Classification — Key Principles

  • Based on displacement of 4 anatomical segments: head, GT, LT, shaft
  • A segment is "displaced" if >1 cm displaced OR >45° angulated
  • 1-Part = minimal displacement regardless of fracture lines (most common — 49%)
  • 2-Part surgical neck fractures are most common displaced pattern (28%)
  • GT fractures — watch for isolated displacement >5 mm (surgical threshold in active patients)
  • 4-Part fractures — highest AVN risk; consider arthroplasty

Segment Deforming Forces

  • Humeral head — balanced rotator cuff pull; tends to extension and external rotation
  • Shaft — pectoralis major pulls anteromedially
  • Greater tuberosity — supraspinatus/infraspinatus pull superoposteriorly
  • Lesser tuberosity — subscapularis pulls medially
Caution

Reliability: Neer classification has poor inter-observer reliability (kappa ~0.4). AO/OTA provides better reproducibility for research.

Neer Type 1

1-Part (Minimal Displacement)

Any number of fracture lines but NO segment meets displacement criteria (<1 cm and <45°). Most common pattern — 49% of proximal humerus fractures, median age 63 years.

  • Periosteum, rotator cuff, and joint capsule maintain cohesion
  • Treatment: sling immobilization × 2–3 weeks, early pendulum exercises
  • Prognosis excellent with appropriate rehabilitation
Neer Type 3 — 28% of fractures

2-Part Surgical Neck Fracture

Shaft displaced relative to head + tuberosities. Most common displaced fracture. Pectoralis major deforms shaft anteriorly/medially.

  • Valgus-impacted pattern — favorable; blood supply via medial periosteal hinge preserved
  • Displacement thresholds for surgery: >1 cm displacement OR >45° angulation, OR >50% cortical contact lost
  • Closed reduction + percutaneous pinning vs. ORIF vs. IMN
  • Assess medial calcar integrity — loss → instability, fixation failure risk
Neer Type 4 — 4% of fractures

2-Part Greater Tuberosity Fracture

GT displaced by supraspinatus/infraspinatus pull — typically superoposterior. May be associated with anterior shoulder dislocation.

  • Surgical threshold: >5 mm displacement in active patients; >3 mm in overhead athletes (AO Surgery Reference)
  • Posterior displacement blocks external rotation; superior migration blocks abduction
  • Fixation: suture anchor fixation, screw fixation, or locking plate
  • Associated rotator cuff tear must be addressed at surgery
Neer Type 5 — <1% of fractures

2-Part Lesser Tuberosity Fracture

Rare. Subscapularis avulsion. Often associated with posterior fracture-dislocation. CT scan essential.

  • Consider posterior dislocation if mechanism is electrocution, seizure, or posterior blow
  • Surgical repair if >1 cm displaced or block to internal rotation
  • Coronally reformatted CT identifies impression fracture (reverse Hill-Sachs)
Neer Types 8–9 — ~10% of fractures

3-Part Fracture

Head + one tuberosity (displaced) + shaft. Most commonly GT + surgical neck (Type 8, 9%). The intact tuberosity rotates the head via its rotator cuff attachment.

  • 3-Part GT + surgical neck: head externally rotated by intact subscapularis
  • 3-Part LT + surgical neck: head internally rotated by intact posterior cuff
  • Generally requires ORIF (locking plate ± suture augmentation)
  • Arthroplasty considered in osteoporotic elderly (>70 yrs) or poor bone quality
  • AVN risk ~3–14% for 3-part fractures
Neer Types 12–14 — ~2–3% of fractures

4-Part Fracture

All four segments displaced. Humeral head isolated from blood supply — AVN rate >13%. Most complex pattern.

  • Valgus-impacted 4-part (Neer 12): medial periosteal hinge preserved → lower AVN risk; ORIF feasible
  • True 4-part with lateral head displacement: high AVN risk; arthroplasty preferred
  • Hemiarthroplasty vs. Reverse TSA based on rotator cuff status and bone quality
  • Reverse TSA favored in elderly (>70 yrs) with poor tuberosity bone
  • Younger patients (<60 yrs): ORIF attempted to preserve native joint
Neer Types 6–7, 13–14

Fracture-Dislocation

Any part fracture with glenohumeral dislocation. Head is completely outside the joint. Axillary view is diagnostic.

  • Anterior (Types 6, 10, 13): most common; GT fracture ± surgical neck
  • Posterior (Types 7, 11, 14): associated with seizure, electrocution; LT fracture common; reverse Hill-Sachs impression
  • Neurovascular injury risk significantly elevated
  • Closed reduction rarely achievable — typically requires ORIF or arthroplasty
  • CT mandatory preoperatively

Non-Operative Treatment

  • Indicated for ~80% of proximal humerus fractures (1-part, minimally displaced)
  • Sling immobilization × 2–3 weeks, then progressive ROM
  • Pendulum exercises begin at 1–2 weeks
  • Active-assisted ROM at 3–4 weeks
  • Full strengthening at 8–12 weeks (bony union)
Clinical Pearl

Monitoring: Repeat radiograph at 1 week to confirm no late displacement (especially valgus-impacted surgical neck and GT fractures). Beware of early loss of reduction.

ORIF — Locking Plate Principles

  • Place plate at ~5–8 mm distal to GT tip and 2 mm posterior to bicipital groove
  • Restore medial calcar support — medial hinge screw or calcar screw reduces varus collapse
  • Avoid superior plate placement → subacromial impingement
  • Suture augmentation of tuberosities to plate reduces construct failure
  • Avoid screw penetration into articular surface — intraoperative axillary fluoroscopic check
  • Subchondral screws must be >5 mm from articular surface

06Surgical Approaches

Approach Walkthrough

Deltopectoral Approach (Primary)

AO Surgery Reference Standard

Deltopectoral Approach — Overview

The workhorse approach for proximal humerus fractures. Internervous plane between deltoid (axillary nerve) and pectoralis major (medial/lateral pectoral nerves). Extensile — can be extended distally for shaft exposure.

  • Indications: ORIF (locking plate, IM nail), fracture-dislocation, arthroplasty
  • Extensile proximally to clavicle and distally along shaft
  • Excellent visualization of lesser tuberosity, subscapularis, and bicipital groove
  • Limited posterior GT visualization — may need deltoid-splitting extension

Patient Positioning

Beach chair (semi-recumbent) position is standard. Head of table elevated 45–60°. Head secured in neutral position, contralateral shoulder padded.

  • Beach chair: gravity assists arm positioning, easy fluoroscopy
  • Table edge at medial scapular border — full shoulder mobility
  • Padded arm holder or assistant holds arm in neutral rotation
  • Fluoroscopy from head — Grashey and axillary views achievable
Caution
Caution: Cerebral hypoperfusion risk in beach chair — maintain MAP >65 mmHg. Communicate with anesthesia.

Skin Incision

Begin at coracoid process and extend distally 10–15 cm toward the deltoid insertion following the deltopectoral groove (lateral to midline of shoulder).

  • Landmark: coracoid tip → anterior axillary fold
  • Incision length adjustable based on fracture complexity
  • Subcutaneous flaps developed medially and laterally
  • Identify deltopectoral groove by fat stripe and cephalic vein

Developing the Deep Interval

The deltopectoral interval is developed by retracting deltoid laterally and pectoralis major medially. Cephalic vein is the key landmark.

  • Cephalic vein runs in groove — retract laterally with deltoid (maintains venous tributaries)
  • Divide clavipectoral fascia lateral to conjoint tendon
  • Retract conjoint tendon (coracobrachialis + short head biceps) medially
  • Self-retaining retractor placed once interval developed
Caution
Avoid: Aggressive medial retraction on conjoint tendon — musculocutaneous nerve enters coracobrachialis 3–8 cm distal to coracoid.

Cephalic Vein & Clavipectoral Fascia

Clavipectoral fascia is incised lateral to the coracobrachialis up to the coracoacromial ligament. Preserve the CA ligament when possible.

  • Mobilize cephalic vein throughout — avoid tearing small perforators
  • If vein torn: ligate and retract; approach unaffected
  • Anterior humeral circumflex vessels visible at inferior surgical neck — protect
  • Bicipital groove identified as key landmark for plate positioning

Subscapularis & Articular Exposure

For arthroplasty or inferior head fragment access, subscapularis takedown is required. Two techniques: lesser tuberosity osteotomy (preferred) or tenotomy 1 cm medial to insertion.

  • LT osteotomy: improved repair strength, earlier ROM; transosseous suture fixation
  • Tenotomy: simpler; repair with sutures through drill holes in bone
  • Axillary nerve palpated at inferior capsule — protect throughout
  • Circumflex vessels ligated if necessary for exposure (arthroplasty)
Caution
Axillary nerve: Palpate inferior capsule before any inferior capsule release. Nerve lies ~2 cm inferior to glenoid rim medially.

Closure

Layered closure. Clavipectoral fascia is not routinely closed. Deltopectoral interval approximated loosely with absorbable sutures.

  • Subscapularis repaired first — transosseous sutures through LT if osteotomy performed
  • Deep subcutaneous layer closed with absorbable suture
  • Skin: subcuticular or staples per surgeon preference
  • Drain: optional; not routinely required
  • Immobilize in sling immediately postoperatively

Anterolateral Deltoid-Splitting Approach

  • Indicated for isolated GT fractures and some surgical neck patterns
  • Split deltoid in line of fibers, 4 cm distal to acromion (DO NOT exceed 5 cm — axillary nerve risk)
  • Suture placed at distal extent of split to prevent propagation
  • Direct access to GT and supraspinatus footprint
  • Less morbidity than full deltopectoral; limited extensibility
Caution

Axillary Nerve Rule: The anterior branch of the axillary nerve crosses <5 cm from the lateral acromion edge. Splitting beyond this risks permanent deltoid denervation.

Caution

Varus Malreduction: Loss of medial calcar support leads to varus collapse and fixation failure. Always restore medial hinge or augment with calcar screw/bone graft. Varus >20° is strongly associated with implant failure.

Clinical Pearl

Arthroplasty Salvage: Reverse TSA is the preferred salvage procedure for failed ORIF or AVN with rotator cuff deficiency in patients >65 years. Tuberosity healing is key determinant of functional outcome in hemiarthroplasty.
