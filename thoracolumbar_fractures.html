The Big Picture

More than 150,000 spine injuries occur in North America each year. 65–90% involve the thoracic and lumbar spine, and ~27% of thoracolumbar injuries carry an associated neurologic deficit.

Epidemiology

  • Spine injuries account for 6% of all fractures and affect 5–30% of polytrauma patients.
  • The thoracolumbar region (T10–L2) is the most commonly affected segment — the transitional zone between the rigid thoracic and flexible lumbar spines.
  • 27% of patients with thoracolumbar injuries have associated neurologic deficits.
  • Bimodal age distribution: peak in males <30 (high-energy) and a second peak in elderly with poor bone quality (low-energy ground-level falls).
  • Up to 20% of polytrauma patients have noncontiguous vertebral fractures — full-spine CT mandatory.
  • Delayed or missed diagnosis occurs in 5–24% and can be neurologically devastating.

Mechanism of Injury

  • MVC 36.7% · Falls from height 31.7%.
  • Force vectors: axial loading (compression), distraction, flexion, extension, shear, rotation — often in combination.
  • Pathophysiology of SCI: acute compression → secondary biochemical cascade (ischemia, free radicals, vasoconstriction, intracellular Ca²⁺ influx, lipid peroxidation, excitotoxins).

Associated Injuries

NTDB analysis shows associated bony and internal organ injuries in 79% of thoracic and 71% of lumbar spine fractures.

  • Thoracic spine fractures: rib cage (47%), lumbar spine (26%), cervical spine (25%), lungs (35%), intracranial (24%).
  • Lumbar spine fractures: rib cage (38%), thoracic spine (22%), pelvic ring (20%), lungs (26%), intracranial (19%).
  • SCI patients face systemic complications: orthostatic hypotension, autonomic dysreflexia, neurogenic bladder/bowel, respiratory failure, disuse osteoporosis, heterotopic ossification.

Patient Evaluation

  • Full spinal precautions from first contact — rigid backboard, cervical collar — until cleared. Remove backboard once imaging done to prevent decubitus ulcers (especially in neurologically impaired patients).
  • ATLS first. Distinguish neurogenic shock (hypotension WITH paradoxical bradycardia) from hypovolemic shock (compensatory tachycardia) — most polytrauma patients have at least a hypovolemic component regardless.
  • Log-roll examination: one person stabilizing the cervical spine plus two for torso/lower extremities. Inspect for soft tissue injury, palpate for tenderness, step-offs, interspinous defects, internal degloving.
  • Bulbocavernosus reflex: absent in spinal shock; once it returns, the absence of motor/sensory function below the level confirms complete SCI. Delayed in conus medullaris/cauda equina injuries. Priapism is an ominous sign of complete sensorimotor deficit.
  • Rectal exam: tone, perianal sensation (sacral sparing = incomplete SCI, positive prognostic indicator).

ISNCSCI / ASIA Neurological Examination

The American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale (AIS) is the universal SCI classification — ideally completed within 72 hours of injury. The ASIA motor score is a 100-point scale (5 key muscle groups × each of 4 extremities × 5/5 grade).

ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS)

  • A — Complete: no sensory or motor function preserved in sacral segments S4–S5.
  • B — Sensory Incomplete: sensory but not motor function preserved below NLI, including S4–S5 (light touch, pin prick, or DAP); no motor function more than 3 levels below the motor level on either side.
  • C — Motor Incomplete: motor function preserved at most caudal sacral segments for VAC OR sensory incomplete with motor sparing >3 levels below ipsilateral motor level. <½ of key muscle functions below NLI graded ≥3.
  • D — Motor Incomplete: ≥½ of key muscle functions below NLI graded ≥3.
  • E — Normal: sensation and motor function normal in all segments, with prior documented deficits.

Imaging

Plain Radiographs

  • No longer the initial screening tool — superseded by CT. Still valuable for upright dynamic assessment and follow-up.
  • Assess sagittal/coronal alignment, rotation, translation, three-column integrity (Denis), vertebral height loss, interpedicular/interspinous widening.
  • Interpedicular widening on AP → burst fracture. Interspinous widening → posterior tension band injury.
  • Unstable injury indicators: >50% vertebral height loss + >30° kyphosis + 2.5 mm translation.
  • Cobb angle quantifies kyphotic deformity.

Computed Tomography (CT)

  • 99% sensitivity — initial screening modality of choice in high-energy trauma.
  • Standard trauma chest-abdomen-pelvis CT is routinely reformatted for spinal assessment. Dedicated spine CT uses 2–3 mm axial slices with sagittal/coronal reformats.
  • Provides fracture propagation, comminution, canal compromise dimensions.
  • Indirect ligamentous signs: spinal translation, widened facet joints, interspinous distance, intervertebral disc space.
  • Ankylosed spines (AS, DISH): any fracture — even nondisplaced — should be regarded as highly unstable and confirmed with MRI when not contraindicated.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

  • Higher sensitivity than CT for PLC, ALL, PLL, intervertebral disc, and neural tissue assessment.
  • Identifies cord injury patterns: hemorrhagic contusion, nonhemorrhagic contusion, epidural hematoma, bone/disc compression, complete transection.
  • Caveat: MRI overestimates PLC instability — combine morphologic assessment (X-ray + CT + MRI) rather than relying on MRI alone.
  • Indicated when neurologic deficit suspected or PLC integrity is unclear after CT.
  • Not routine for every thoracolumbar fracture (cost, accessibility).

Applied Anatomy

The thoracic, thoracolumbar, and lumbosacral regions differ in biomechanics, neural anatomy, and injury vulnerability — these differences shape treatment principles.

Three Functional Regions

RegionLevelsCurvatureKey Features
Thoracic T2–T10 Kyphotic 20–50° Rigid — coronally-oriented facets, ribs, thin discs. Narrow canal with little reserve. Anterior spinal artery vascular supply vulnerable.
Thoracolumbar T11–L2 Transitional Most vulnerable to fractures and dislocations. Spinal cord ends at L1; conus medullaris between T11–L1. Conus medullaris syndrome possible.
Lumbosacral L3–S1 Lordotic 40–60° Flexible — sagittal facets. Wide canal with cauda equina (no central nervous system). Tolerates canal compromise better.

Vertebral Body Morphology

  • Vertebral bodies progressively enlarge caudally.
  • Lumbar pedicles gradually increase L1 → L5.
  • Thoracic pedicles: decrease T1 → T4 (T4–T5 narrowest), then increase T5 → T12.
  • T12 pedicles often wider than L1 and L2.

Center of Gravity

  • Thoracic spine: CoG anterior → compressive force on vertebral bodies, tensile force on posterior elements.
  • Lumbar spine: weight-bearing axis posterior → tensile across vertebral bodies, compressive across facets. Limits kyphotic forces.

Posterior Ligamentous Complex (PLC)

The PLC is the posterior tension band — its disruption is the hallmark of mechanical instability in flexion-distraction injuries.

  • Facet joint capsules
  • Ligamentum flavum
  • Interspinous ligaments
  • Supraspinous ligaments
  • Posterior bony attachments (lamina, spinous process, pedicles)

Clinical Pearl — PLC Failure

PLC disruption alone (Holdsworth) or combined with middle column failure (Denis) defines acute instability. Holdsworth's posterior tension band concept remains valid today.

Neural Anatomy by Level

  • Spinal cord ends at L1 in adults.
  • Conus medullaris: spindle-shaped distal cord between T11–L1. Injury → conus medullaris syndrome (CMS): back pain → legs, mixed upper/lower motor neuron signs, bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction, saddle anesthesia.
  • Cauda equina: floating nerve roots below L1 within the lumbar canal — peripheral nerves, more resilient to injury.

Vascular Supply to the Cord

  • Anterior spinal artery — major thoracic cord supply. Disruption causes anterior cord syndrome / cord ischemia from minor canal compromise.
  • Artery of Adamkiewicz: the dominant radiculomedullary artery. Typical origin on LEFT between T8–T10 but can range T7–L4. Critical surgical consideration for anterior approaches and segmental vessel ligation.

Surgical Caution — Adamkiewicz Artery

During anterior approaches, segmental vessels should be temporarily clamped and checked with neuromonitoring before division. Ligate close to the aorta to preserve collateral circulation to the cord.

Classification Systems

Spinal stability (per White & Panjabi) is the ability of the spine under physiologic loads to prevent neural injury, deformity, and pain. Classification systems exist to translate biomechanics into treatment.

Historical Evolution

SystemYearKey Concept

McAfee1983First CT-based classification — wedge compression, stable burst, unstable burst, Chance, flexion-distraction, translational. Ordered by increasing instability.
Denis1983Three-column model. Acute instability requires posterior AND middle column disruption. Compression, burst, seatbelt-type, fracture-dislocation.
Original AO1994Type A (compression), B (distraction/bending), C (rotation). 53 subtypes — too complex for routine clinical use.
TLICS2005Scoring system: morphology + PLC + neuro. Score ≥5 → operative; ≤3 → nonoperative; 4 → surgeon discretion.
Load-Sharing (McCormack)1994Predicts need for anterior column support. LSS >6 → long-segment or anteroposterior reconstruction.
Revised AOSpine2013Current standard — see below.

Revised AOSpine Thoracolumbar Classification (2013)

Load-Sharing Classification (McCormack)

Helps determine the need for anterior column support. Points are assigned based on:

  1. Extent of vertebral body comminution
  2. Apposition of bony fragments
  3. Degree of focal kyphosis

LSS Decision Threshold

  • LSS ≤6: short-segment posterior fixation sufficient
  • LSS >6: long-segment posterior or combined anteroposterior reconstruction needed

Treatment Principles

The two primary surgical indications are neurologic deficit and spinal instability. Most other treatment debates revolve around the A3/A4 burst fracture without neurologic compromise.

General Treatment Goals

  • Maintain acceptable mechanical stability and spinal alignment
  • Provide adequate neurologic decompression when indicated
  • Protect neural elements against secondary injury
  • Restore alignment using the shortest possible construct
  • Allow early mobilization

Hemodynamic & Medical Management

Mean Arterial Pressure Goals

  • Target MAP ≥85 mm Hg in many institutions (some use ≥80 mm Hg); maintain for 48–72 hours after admission and 24–48 hours postoperatively.
  • Evidence is mixed — incomplete SCI patients may benefit more than complete SCI. Vasopressors can cause cardiac adverse events.
  • Spinal cord perfusion pressure ≥50 mm Hg has been associated with neurologic recovery.

High-Dose Methylprednisolone (NASCIS II/III)

  • 30 mg/kg bolus → 5.4 mg/kg/h × 23 hours if started within 8 hours of injury (24-hour protocol if within 3 hours; 48-hour if 3–8 hours).
  • Controversial. CNS/AANS 2013 recommended against; AOSpine NA/International/AANS-CNS more recent guidance cites weak evidence and re-allows 24-hour infusion as an option.
  • Risks: GI complications, infection, pancreatitis, pulmonary, death — particularly in elderly with comorbidities.
  • NOT recommended for penetrating SCI (no benefit, significant harm).

Nonoperative Treatment

Indications

  • Mechanically stable fractures (most type A injuries)
  • Neurologically intact
  • No significant deformity
  • Purely osseous Chance (B1) — can be braced in hyperextension

Bracing

  • T7–L3: TLSO (Jewett hyperextension for sagittal plane injuries; clamshell for coronal/rotational)
  • ≥T6: CTLSO (cervicothoracolumbosacral orthosis)
  • Below L3: HTLSO (hip-thoracolumbosacral orthosis) with thigh extension
  • Duration: typically 6 weeks–3 months; restrict lifting >10 lb, no bending/twisting; wear at all times out of bed
  • Obtain upright in-brace radiographs to confirm alignment under physiologic loads
  • Bailey 2014 RCT: A3 burst fractures (T11–L3, kyphosis <35°, neuro intact, <60 yo) had equivalent outcomes with or without brace at 3 months and 2 years.

Timing of Surgery

  • STASCIS: decompression <24 hours after SCI was safe and associated with ≥2 grades AIS improvement at 6 months.
  • Early thoracic stabilization reduces ventilator days, ICU days, and respiratory morbidity. Lumbar fractures: only LOS reduction shown.
  • Early intervention risks: incomplete resuscitation, missed injuries, hemodynamic instability.

Type B · Tension Band Injuries — Treatment

SubtypeTreatment
B1 (Chance)Hyperextension brace if medically optimal. Operative: short-segment mono-osseous posterior compression construct. Hardware can be removed after healing to preserve motion. MIS-friendly.
B2Operative — posterior instrumented fusion ≥1 level above and below. NEVER anterior-only. Consider anterior reconstruction if associated severe A3/A4.
B3Operative — long fusion (≥2–3 levels above/below) in ankylosed spine. Avoid hyperextension during transport. Prepositioning neuromonitoring. MRI for epidural hematoma (~10% in ankylosed spine).

Type C · Translation — Treatment

  • All operative regardless of neuro status.
  • Posterior approach first — multilevel instrumentation.
  • Realignment often achieves indirect canal decompression.
  • Second-stage anterior reconstruction guided by CT assessment of anterior column integrity.
  • ≥75% have associated neurologic deficit on presentation.

Posterior Approach — Core Principles

  • Most familiar approach; extensile; allows multilevel correction of kyphosis (B) and translation (C).
  • Pedicle screw and rod fixation is instrumentation of choice — biomechanically superior 3-column fixation.
  • Short-segment fixation (1 level above/below): reserve for young, healthy, good bone stock, no metabolic disease. Higher failure rate without anterior column support.
  • Long-segment fixation (≥2 levels above/below): osteoporotic bone, thoracic injuries, severe comminution.
  • Ligamentotaxis: distraction across pedicle screws can decompress canal up to 50% via annulus/PLL traction. Ineffective if annulus disrupted or fragment rotated 180°.

Anterior Approach — Indications

  • Bony retropulsion causing neurologic deficit
  • Severe canal compromise where ligamentotaxis fails
  • Kyphosis >30°
  • Severe vertebral body comminution
  • Traumatic disc herniation at cord/conus level
  • Subacute fractures where ligamentotaxis is ineffective

When to AVOID Anterior-Only Fixation

  • Osteoporotic bone — high subsidence/cutout risk
  • B2/C injuries (PLC injury) — insufficient mechanical advantage
  • Severe instability requiring multilevel fixation — exposure limits

Combined Anteroposterior Approach

  • For severely unstable injuries where neither alone provides adequate stability.
  • Costotransversectomy can achieve circumferential decompression and stabilization through a single posterior approach (T2–L1). Complication rate ~15% (up to 57% reported) — experienced surgeons only.

Minimally Invasive Surgery (MIS)

  • Reduced blood loss, soft tissue injury, infection rates.
  • Best candidates: B1 fractures, minimally displaced B3 (ankylosed spine), burst fractures without major decompression need.
  • Kyphoplasty can augment anterior column in severely comminuted bodies.
  • Fusion ability may be compromised — but healing without fusion of injured segment is acceptable outcome.

Osteoporotic Vertebral Fractures (Special Consideration)

  • ~50% of all osteoporosis-related fractures involve the spine.
  • Nonoperative first-line: bracing (TLSO — controversial efficacy), activity modification, calcitonin × 4 weeks (AAOS moderate strength), bisphosphonates for secondary prevention.
  • Cement augmentation (vertebroplasty / kyphoplasty): conflicting evidence. 2018 Cochrane found no benefit over placebo. AAOS strong recommendation against routine VP. Consider only in subset with severe persistent pain after >6 weeks conservative management.
  • Operative indications: neurologic deficit, progressive painful deformity, nonunion.
  • Surgical adjuncts: cement-augmented pedicle screws, anterior column reconstruction, adjacent-segment vertebroplasty (limited benefit for PJK).
  • Avoid anterior-only constructs in metabolic bone disease.

Surgical Approaches

Pedicle screw–rod posterior instrumentation is the workhorse for thoracolumbar trauma. Anterior corpectomy provides direct ventral decompression and load-sharing reconstruction when posterior alone is insufficient.

Posterior Midline Approach & Pedicle Screw Fixation

Pedicle Screw Starting Points

  • Thoracic: junction of the lateral pars / superior facet and the base of the transverse process.
  • Lumbar: junction of the superior facet and corresponding transverse process (at the mammillary process).
  • Confirm levels fluoroscopically. T4–T5 pedicles are the narrowest — use template if available.

Side Selection — Critical Rule

  • Above T11: RIGHT side preferred (avoid cardiac structures)
  • T10 and below (thoracolumbar): LEFT side preferred (avoid liver, easier IVC manipulation)
  • L5–S1 anterior approach: Pfannenstiel or paramedian on LEFT (avoid right common iliac vein)

Pre-incision Pearls

  • Lateral decubitus on beanbag/pelvic pads; upper arm forward — avoid brachial plexus tension
  • Incision typically two rib levels cranial to fractured vertebra
  • For lumbosacral: Pfannenstiel or paramedian supine; identify and protect superior hypogastric sympathetic plexus at L5–S1 to prevent retrograde ejaculation

Costotransversectomy (Posterolateral Circumferential)

  • Used T2–L1 to achieve circumferential decompression & stabilization through a single posterior incision.
  • Avoids thoracotomy/morbidity of separate anterior approach.
  • Disadvantages: technical difficulty, blood loss, sacrifice of a thoracic nerve root, retraction near the cord. Complication rate ~15%, reported up to 57%.
  • Reserve for experienced surgeons in well-resourced centers (ICU backup).

Key Reduction Techniques

  • Cantilever reduction: contour rod into desired alignment, secure cephalad screws first, then translate caudal end of rod anteriorly to engage caudal screws — uses injury-level facet as fulcrum. Effective for kyphosis correction.
  • Distraction (ligamentotaxis): indirect canal decompression up to 50% via annulus/PLL tension. Cautious use in neurologically intact patients.
  • Polyaxial reduction screws: independent lordosis restoration and vertebral height correction.
  • Direct decompression: laminectomy + facetectomy → angled curette mobilizes retropulsed fragment anteriorly into the corpectomy defect.

Implant Adjuncts in Osteoporotic Bone

  • Cement-augmented pedicle screws (via vertebral body injection or cannulated/fenestrated screws)
  • Larger-diameter screws; posterior hooks to protect screw fixation
  • SAIF (Stent-Screw-Assisted Internal Fixation): vertebral body stents + balloon expansion + cannulated/fenestrated pedicle screws
  • Anterior column reconstruction with cage/structural graft to load-share
  • Adjacent-segment vertebroplasty: may reduce PJK at 2 years but no benefit at 5 years

Complications & Adverse Outcomes

Postoperative infection (~5.5%) and instrumentation failure requiring revision (~4.4%) are the most clinically significant complications. Other complications occur at similar rates in both operative and nonoperative cohorts.

Common Complications

ComplicationIncidenceKey Points
Surgical site infection ~5.5%, up to 10% Intrawound vancomycin powder reduces incidence. Aggressive debridement of devitalized tissue is critical. Leave bone graft in place if possible (essential for fusion).
Instrumentation failure / loss of fixation ~4.4% Higher with short-segment fixation of burst fractures lacking anterior support. Consider revision with longer construct ± anterior reconstruction.
Neurologic deterioration ~1% posterior procedures Causes: direct injury during instrumentation, manipulation, positioning, loss of reduction. Use neuromonitoring routinely; baseline signals before risky positioning.
Traumatic durotomy ~12% of TL fractures Most common with C-type translation injuries and A3/A4 burst with longitudinal lamina fractures (up to 19% in this subset). Repair watertight with running suture ± sealant patch.
CSF leak / pseudomeningocele ~2.1% after repair Bed rest until asymptomatic, then gradual head-of-bed elevation. Persistent: lumbar subarachnoid drain or revision repair.
Symptomatic DVT / PE 1–5% Sequential compression devices for all trauma patients. Chemoprophylaxis 24–48 h postoperatively per recent consensus (limited strength, strong consensus). IVC filter if absolute contraindication to chemoprophylaxis.
Late posttraumatic kyphosis Common long-term Surgery indicated for pain, progressive deficit, postural difficulty + focal kyphosis ≥25°. Smith-Peterson/Ponte osteotomy ~10–15° per level; pedicle subtraction osteotomy up to 30° single-level.
Concomitant visceral / vascular injury Up to 40% in B and C Great vessel injuries common in B1/B2/B3/C; bowel injuries common in flexion-distraction. Pedicle screw misplacement may injure great vessels intraoperatively.

Cement Augmentation Complications

  • Cement extravasation: up to 90% of patients — disc (45%), paravertebral (35%), epidural (20%), prevertebral (18%). Worse with low cement viscosity, high volumes, severe fractures.
  • Neurologic complications from leakage: 0.1% — may require decompression and reconstruction.
  • Cement embolization: small cured fragments or monomer with distal polymerization. Symptomatic PE rates up to 26% reported. Rare fatal pulmonary/systemic embolism. Fat embolization causing hemodynamic changes also possible.
  • Adjacent-level fractures: ~20% develop new fractures 6–12 months post-treatment, similar rate to nonoperative. Risk factors: lower BMI, lower BMD, intradiscal cement leakage.

SCI-Specific Systemic Complications

  • Cardiovascular: orthostatic hypotension, autonomic dysreflexia
  • Pulmonary: respiratory failure, pulmonary edema
  • Urinary: neurogenic bladder, kidney failure, recurrent UTI
  • Musculoskeletal: spasticity, atrophy, disuse osteoporosis, heterotopic ossification
  • GI: neurogenic bowel dysfunction, ileus

Penetrating Spinal Injuries — Brief Notes

  • ~50% involve thoracic spine, ~33% lumbar. Civilian GSI = 3rd most common cause of SCI.
  • ~90% have associated organ injuries — ATLS first.
  • Steroids NOT recommended for penetrating SCI.
  • Stabilization rarely needed for low-energy civilian GSI even with 2–3 column involvement.
  • Surgery for: neurologic compression (especially incomplete, progressive), CSF leak, bullet removal (T12–L4 fragments may improve motor recovery; above T11 no benefit shown).
  • Antibiotics: 24 h is sufficient even with GI tract injury (recent evidence). Broad-spectrum for transabdominal trajectories.
  • Lead toxicity: very rare from spinal fragments; intradiscal location highest risk. Monitor serum lead levels; consider removal if >50 mcg/dL symptomatic or >80 mcg/dL asymptomatic.
