Patellar Fractures

The largest sesamoid, the fulcrum of the extensor mechanism — where restoring articular congruity and the tension side of the patella governs the whole reconstruction.

Source: Rockwood & Green’s Fractures in Adults, 10th ed. · Ch. 60 — Patellar Fractures & Extensor Mechanism · AO Foundation Surgery Reference

01Overview & Assessment

Roughly 1% of all fractures, with a bimodal age distribution and a rising incidence as bone quality declines. Pattern is dictated by mechanism, age, bone quality, and knee flexion at impact.

Mechanism of injury

  • Direct — blow to the anterior knee (fall, dashboard). Low- or high-energy; tends to produce comminuted/stellate patterns with chondral impaction against the condyles.
  • Indirect — violent eccentric quadriceps pull on a flexed knee; propagates through retinaculum, produces transverse patterns and loss of active extension.
  • Most fractures are a combination — a direct blow plus quadriceps contraction plus joint impaction.
Associated injuries

High-energy mechanisms carry a substantial associated-injury burden. Open fractures reach association rates approaching 80%, with up to 44% ipsilateral lower-extremity fracture and ~25% PCL injury in operative series. A high-energy patella fracture should trigger a full ipsilateral-limb survey.

Signs & symptoms

  • Anterior knee pain, hemarthrosis, palpable inter-fragmentary defect.
  • Test the extensor mechanism — straight-leg raise or extension of a partially flexed knee against gravity.
  • Aspirate a tense hemarthrosis and inject local anaesthetic if pain prevents examination.
  • Ability to extend does not exclude a fracture — it means the retinacular sleeve is intact.
  • Any laceration/abrasion → saline load test (~150 mL ± methylene blue) to exclude an open fracture / traumatic arthrotomy.
Absent effusion — a trap

The absence of a large effusion in the presence of a palpable bony defect should raise concern, not reassure — it suggests the retinaculum is torn and the hemarthrosis has decompressed into the soft tissues.

Imaging

  • AP, lateral, and axial/tangential views; contralateral views help distinguish variants.
  • Lateral defines pattern, extensor disruption and patellar height.
  • Axial view is essential for vertical/marginal lines and osteochondral defects easily missed on AP.
  • Radiographs typically underestimate comminution.
  • Insall–Salvati ratio (tendon length : patella length): normal 1.02 ± 0.13.
  • Ratio >1 → patella alta (patellar-tendon disruption); <1 → patella baja (quadriceps disruption).
  • CT for stress fracture, nonunion, comminution planning — but often upgrades apparent displacement beyond the literature base.
  • MRI for suspected extensor / osteochondral injury.
Masqueraders

A bipartite/tripartite patella (~8% incidence, usually bilateral, classically superolateral) has smooth corticated margins and is not tender — contralateral radiographs confirm. A truly unilateral bipartite patella is rare and may represent an old avulsion.

02Pathoanatomy

The patella is the body’s largest sesamoid. Its two biomechanical jobs — linking the quadriceps and patellar tendons, and displacing the extensor moment arm — explain why preservation matters.

Osseous anatomy

  • Proximal margin = basis; rounded inferior margin = apex. Ossifies at 2–3 yr.
  • Proximal three-fourths covered by thick articular cartilage (≥1 cm); the distal pole is bare — so many distal-pole fractures are effectively extra-articular.
  • A vertical ridge divides medial and lateral facets; a further medial ridge defines the odd facet.
  • Wiberg classification of facet morphology: Type I equal facets; Type II medial < lateral; Type III convex, small medial facet.

Arterial blood supply

  • Six arteries form a rich dorsal peripatellar plexus — supreme geniculate, four geniculates, and the anterior tibial recurrent.
  • Intraosseous supply enters the mid-anterior body and distal pole and perfuses distal → proximal (retrograde).
  • This retrograde pattern explains the risk of proximal-fragment osteonecrosis after transverse fracture.

Soft tissue & the extensor mechanism

The extensor mechanism = quadriceps tendon + medial/lateral retinacula + patella + patellar tendon. The retinacula insert directly onto the tibia, so they can drive active extension even with a fractured patella.

  • Quadriceps: rectus femoris (superficial, central), vastus lateralis (~30°), vastus medialis longus/obliquus (VMO 50–55°), vastus intermedius (into superior base).
  • MPFL — extracapsular VMO continuation; the major restraint to lateral patellar translation (>50% of medial restraining force).
  • Patellar tendon — ~5 cm, from apex to tibial tubercle, a continuation of central rectus fibres.

Biomechanics — why we preserve bone

  • Extensor forces can exceed 6,000 N (up to 8× body weight); patellofemoral contact reaches 3–7× body weight.
  • The patella’s displacement function increases the extensor moment arm — providing the extra torque for the final 15° of terminal extension (twice the torque of the first 15°).
  • Subtotal excision studies: keep a fragment ≥ three-fourths of the proximal patella to preserve mechanical advantage — the basis for favouring partial over total patellectomy.

03Classification

Begin clinically: displaced or not. Displaced = >3 mm fragment separation or >2 mm articular incongruity. Then describe by geometry and location. The OTA/AO 34 system standardises this for research.

Why displacement matters

Displacement is highly correlated with articular incongruity. CT frequently reveals more comminution and displacement than plain films — useful for planning, but be cautious: the outcome literature is built on radiographic thresholds, so CT can push surgical indications beyond the evidence.

04Management

The pivot is the extensor mechanism and articular congruity. Goals: preserve patellar bone (linking & displacement), restore congruity, and restore a competent, strong extensor mechanism.

Non-operative

Indications

  • Intact extensor mechanism
  • <3 mm articular incongruity
  • Severe comorbidity / non-ambulatory

Technique

  • 4–6 wk extension bracing / cylinder cast; early SLR & isometrics.
  • ROM once callus is radiographically evident; re-image early.

Operative

Indications

  • >3 mm articular incongruity
  • Loss of active extension / incompetent extensor mechanism
  • Open fracture; osteochondral loose bodies

Options

  • ORIF — tension band, cannulated-screw TB, or plate(s)/screws
  • Partial patellectomy + tendon repair
  • Total patellectomy (last resort)
Non-operative outcomes

In Böström’s series, 98% of minimally-displaced fractures (<3 mm) treated non-operatively had good-to-excellent results, with no relationship between <3 mm incongruity and later arthritis over ~9 yr. Other series report failure rates <5%.

Fixation — principles & construct selection

The anterior tension-band principle converts anterior tensile force from the quadriceps into compression at the articular surface during knee flexion. Wire must sit hard against bone with minimal interposed tendon.

Counsel the patient

Meta-analysis reoperation rate is 33.6%, most commonly for symptomatic implants. Even “successful” fixation leaves residual dysfunction — up to 80% report some anterior knee pain with daily activities and measurable extension-strength deficits at one year.

05Surgical Approach & Technique

A midline longitudinal extensile incision with full-thickness flaps is the versatile default. Walk through the modified anterior tension band / cannulated-screw construct step by step.

The incision

A midline longitudinal incision centred over the patella extends proximally and distally for revision or future arthroplasty. Expose the articular surface through the fracture or retinacular rents; a lateral parapatellar arthrotomy with eversion (Gardner) gives direct articular visualisation when the retinaculum is intact.

Pitfalls & prevention
  • Wound breakdown → avoid subcutaneous flaps.
  • Early wire failure → no proud cannulated-screw tips.
  • Malreduction → don’t trust fluoroscopy/dorsal cortex alone; palpate or visualise the articular surface.
  • K-wire migration → bend ends 180° posteriorly, impact into patella.
  • Intra-articular penetration → drill wires retrograde through the fracture site.

06Complications & Outcomes

Reoperation is common and should be part of the consent conversation. Most reoperations are for symptomatic hardware rather than fixation failure.

Symptomatic implant

Commonest reoperation
  • K-wires and any implant prominence >5 mm are modifiable risk factors.
  • Braided cable/suture and cannulated-screw TB reduce the rate (~50% lower vs K-wires).
  • Place wire twists superiorly, bend ends posteriorly into deep tissue.

Reoperation (overall)

~33.6% (meta-analysis)
  • Higher with tension-band constructs than screw/interfragmentary techniques.
  • Loss of reduction >2 mm reported in up to 22% of modified TB series.

Loss of reduction / fixation failure

Early hazard
  • Avoid over-tensioning (articular gapping / wire breakage).
  • Repair retinaculum to augment construct strength.
  • Delay ROM if fixation or soft tissues are tenuous.

Post-traumatic arthritis

Common, often subclinical
  • Grade II/III chondral change found at hardware removal in ~73% despite good knee scores.
  • No relationship with <3 mm incongruity over long-term follow-up.

Osteonecrosis

Retrograde blood supply
  • Proximal fragment at risk given distal-to-proximal intraosseous perfusion.
  • Often revascularises; usually managed expectantly.

Infection / open fractures

6–9% are open
  • Urgent I&D, antibiotics, tetanus; immediate fixation reasonable for type I–II after debridement.
  • Deep infection uncommon in low-grade open injuries treated promptly.
Outcome measurement caveat

No validated patella-specific outcome score exists; the Böstman scale is most reported. Comparability across series is poor because results are usually grouped by construct rather than by fracture pattern — read published success rates with that limitation in mind.
