01 — Introduction

Overview & Epidemiology

Clavicle fractures are common injuries in young, active individuals, accounting for 2.6–4% of all fractures. Males are more frequently affected up to age 60, with a peak incidence under 25 years. Annual incidence is estimated at 29–64 per 100,000 persons.

The majority (80–85%) occur in the midshaft where the bone is thinnest and least protected by muscle or ligament. Distal third fractures comprise 15–20%, typically in elderly patients from simple falls. Medial third fractures are rare (0–5%) and associated with high-energy trauma — concomitant injuries occur in up to 80% of cases with a 20% mortality from head and chest injuries.

Historical Context

Despite a long history of nonoperative treatment, modern RCTs clearly show displaced midshaft fractures have nonunion rates of 14–24% when treated conservatively — far higher than previously reported. The paradigm has shifted toward selective operative fixation based on patient and fracture factors.

Treatment should be individualized. Most clavicle fractures can and should be treated nonoperatively. Operative fixation has a relatively narrow risk–benefit ratio and should be reserved for specific indications. Shared decision-making incorporating fracture morphology, patient function, and expectations is essential.

02 — Pathoanatomy

Anatomy & Biomechanics

Bony Anatomy

The clavicle is an S-shaped bone — anteriorly convex medially and anteriorly concave laterally. Cross-sectional shape changes from flat (lateral) to tubular (middle) to prismatic (medial). The flat-to-tubular junction is a stress riser, explaining the high incidence of midshaft fractures. It is the only bony articulation between the axial skeleton and the upper extremity.

AO

Cross-sectional anatomy of the clavicle along its lateral to medial course: flat laterally, tubular in the middle, prismatic medially. The junction between flat and tubular regions acts as a stress riser.

Neurovascular Anatomy

The subclavian artery and vein and brachial plexus pass posterosuperior to anteroinferior between the first rib and clavicle at the medial-middle third junction. The subclavian vein lies within 1 cm of the posterior cortex of the medial half. In the middle third, the artery and vein lie within 2 cm posteroinferiorly. Care must be exercised when using sharp instruments medially.

The supraclavicular nerves (C3/C4) cross the clavicle superficially from medial to lateral in three branches. Division causes numbness below the incision — warn patients preoperatively.

AO

Subclavian artery, vein, and brachial plexus in relation to the clavicle. Neurovascular structures pass beneath the medial-middle third junction.

AO

The sternocleidomastoid muscle inserts on the medial clavicle. The lateral fragment displaces inferiorly and medially under gravity and pectoralis/deltoid pull.

Muscular Anatomy & Deforming Forces

The sternocleidomastoid inserts superiorly on the medial third, elevating the proximal fragment. The distal fragment is displaced inferiorly (deltoid + gravity), anteriorly and medially (pectoralis major). This results in shoulder "ptosis" — a short, droopy, medially rotated shoulder girdle with scapular protraction.

AO

Typical fracture displacement: the distal fragment translates anteriorly, medially, and inferiorly. The medial fragment is relatively fixed. Shortening results from overlapping fragments.

Ligamentous Anatomy

The coracoclavicular (CC) ligaments — trapezoid (lateral) and conoid (medial) — arise from the coracoid and resist superior displacement of the lateral clavicle. Their integrity is critical in lateral third fracture decision-making. The AC joint capsule, thickened superiorly, primarily resists AP displacement.

Medially, the posterior SC capsule is the primary constraint to anterior/posterior translation. The costoclavicular ligaments resist inferior angulation of the medial clavicle.

03 — Assessment & Risk Factors

Mechanism & Risk Factors

Mechanism of Injury

The most common mechanism is a direct blow to the point of the shoulder from a fall (bicycling, motorcycling, sports). Less commonly, a fall on the outstretched hand. High-energy mechanisms (MVA, extreme sports) produce more comminuted fractures. Traction injuries (industrial, machinery) cause distracted, wide-gap fractures — prompt investigation for brachial plexus and vascular injury.

M
Most Common

Direct shoulder blow

Fall on point of shoulder — bicycle accidents predominant in 25–65 yr age group. Produces typical overlapping midshaft fracture.

H
High Energy

Motor vehicle accident

Associated injuries in 80% of polytrauma patients. Rib fractures, pneumothorax, glenoid/scapular fractures. High index of suspicion required.

T
Rare

Traction injury

Arm caught in machinery or fixed during impact. Wide distracted fracture gap. High risk of brachial plexus and vascular injury — CT angiography indicated.

⚠ Alert — Medial Fractures

Medial clavicle fractures result from high-energy trauma with concomitant injuries in up to 80% and 20% mortality from head/chest injuries. CT scanning is mandatory. Consider posterior SC dislocation in young patients (medial physis fuses ~age 25).

Risk Factors for Poor Outcome (Nonoperative)

FactorClinical Significance
Shortening >1.5–2 cm

Most consistently reported prognostic factor for nonunion and malunion. Verify clinically with tape measure.

Complete displacement

No cortical contact between fragments. Associated with nonunion rates of up to 21% in comminuted patterns.

Increasing comminution

>3 fragments associated with higher nonunion. Comminuted pattern is Robinson 2B type.

Female sex

Lower bone mass and different fracture biology contribute to higher nonunion rates in certain series.

Age

Older age is a prognostic factor for nonunion. Children and adolescents have inherently better remodeling potential.

04 — Clinical Assessment

History & Physical Examination

History

Determine mechanism, energy level, dominant hand, occupation, and functional demands (overhead work, throwing, elite sports). Screen for contraindications to surgery: drug/alcohol abuse, untreated psychiatric conditions, uncontrolled seizures. Assess patient expectations regarding cosmesis and timeline.

If mechanism is trivial, investigate for pathologic fracture — lytic lesions, metabolic bone disease, myeloma, metastases. Stress fractures occur in bodybuilders and weightlifters with repetitive loading.

Physical Examination

Inspection
Deformity & Skin
Shoulder "ptosis" — droopy, medially rotated shoulder girdle. Scapular protraction visible from behind. Assess skin blanching over medial fragment (impending open fracture — urgent fixation). Posterior abrasion typical in bicycle falls.
Palpation
Shortening Measurement
Measure sternal notch to AC joint bilaterally. Difference >1.5–2 cm is a relative operative indication. Note: ~30% of individuals have physiologic asymmetry of up to 5 mm. Radiographs tend to overestimate shortening.
Neurovascular
Mandatory Full Assessment
Complete neurologic and vascular examination of the upper limb before any intervention. Pre-existing deficits not identified preoperatively may be attributed incorrectly to surgery — with prognostic and medicolegal implications.
Associated Injuries
Polytrauma Screen
In polytrauma: check for pneumothorax, rib fractures (60% incidence), scapular/glenoid fractures ("floating shoulder"), proximal humeral fractures. A clavicle fracture on chest X-ray in a trauma patient should prompt systematic search for other injuries.
05 — Radiographic Evaluation

Imaging Workup

First Line
AP + Cephalad-Tilted Clavicle Views
Upright radiographs are mandatory — gravity demonstrates maximal deformity. Standard AP plus 15–40° cephalad tilt to avoid thoracic cage overlap. If only one view, prefer the tilted view. Chest X-ray allows comparison of both clavicles for shortening assessment.
Lateral Third
Zanca View
15° cephalad tilt centered on AC joint. Taken without arm support (unsupported to show true displacement). Stress views (2.5–4.5 kg wrist weight) to assess CC ligament integrity and guide fixation choice. CT if intra-articular extension suspected.
Medial Third
CT Scan — Mandatory
Plain radiographs unreliable for medial fractures. CT distinguishes medial physeal fractures (common <25 yr) from true SC dislocations. Identifies posterior displacement impinging on mediastinal structures.
Polytrauma
Repeat Upright Views
Initial supine trauma X-rays underestimate displacement (89% increase in displacement on upright films — Backus et al.). Repeat upright clavicle X-rays as soon as patient allows. Consider CT for floating shoulder (glenoid neck + clavicle).
Key Radiographic Parameters

Assess: location (medial/middle/lateral third), displacement (complete vs partial), comminution (>3 fragments), shortening (>2 cm on chest X-ray = relative operative indication), intra-articular extension, associated injuries. Progressive displacement can occur in early weeks due to ongoing muscle traction — consider repeat films at 1 week for borderline cases.

06 — Classification

Classification Systems

Robinson Classification (Preferred)

Based on prospective analysis of >1,000 patients. Divides clavicle into thirds with prognostically validated subtypes. Note: differs from Allman numbering — medial = Type 1, middle = Type 2, lateral = Type 3.

TypeDescriptionFeatures
1A/1B

Medial (0–5%)

1A: undisplaced/minimally displaced. 1B: displaced / SC involvement. High-energy, associated injuries common.

2A1

Middle, cortical alignment

Cortex intact, no comminution. Low nonunion risk.

2A2

Middle, angulated

Angulated but cortex still in contact. Moderate risk.

2B1

Middle, simple/wedge, displaced

No cortical contact. Significant shortening. Higher nonunion risk.

2B2

Middle, comminuted, displaced

Highest nonunion risk (up to 21%). Strong operative indication.

3A/3B

Lateral (15–20%)

3A: undisplaced, CC ligaments intact. 3B: displaced, CC ligaments disrupted from medial fragment — highest nonunion risk in lateral type.

Neer Classification — Lateral Third

TypeDescription & Significance
Type I

CC ligaments intact. Low displacement, low nonunion risk. Nonoperative treatment appropriate.

Type IIA (Rockwood)

Both conoid and trapezoid attached to distal fragment. Medial fragment free — significant instability and nonunion risk.

Type IIB (Rockwood)

Conoid detached from medial fragment. Only trapezoid on distal fragment. Most unstable — operative fixation indicated.

Type III

Extension into AC joint. AC arthritis may develop. Usually treated nonoperatively initially.

OTA/AO Classification (2018 Update)

Clavicle = segment 15. Divided into medial metaphysis (15.1), diaphysis (15.2), and lateral metaphysis (15.3) using the AO "rule of squares." Diaphysis subtypes: simple (15.2-B1), wedge (15.2-B2), complex (15.2-B3).

07 — Management

Treatment Options

Operative Indications

Absolute

Open fracture / impending open fracture with skin compromise. Vascular injury requiring repair. Progressive neurologic deficit.

Fracture Factors

Displacement >2 cm. Shortening >2 cm. Comminution >3 fragments. Segmental fracture. Obvious clinical deformity. Scapular winging/malposition at presentation.

Associated Injury

Ipsilateral upper extremity fractures. Multiple ipsilateral upper rib fractures. "Floating shoulder" (glenoid neck + clavicle). Bilateral clavicle fractures.

Patient Factors

Polytrauma requiring early UL weight-bearing. Elite athlete or self-employed (rapid return to function). High-demand overhead worker.

Non-Operative

  • Simple arm sling — preferred over figure-of-eight (less pain, better compliance)
  • Sling comfort for 1–2 weeks, then pendulum exercises
  • Non-weight-bearing ROM to horizontal at 2–4 weeks
  • Radiographs at 6 weeks — assess callus formation
  • Strengthening from 6 weeks if satisfactory union
  • Consider repeat X-ray at 1 week for borderline displaced fractures
  • Outcomes plateau at 1 year post-injury

Operative — Implant Options

  • Plate fixation (gold standard) — precontoured anatomic plates preferred. Superior or anteroinferior application.
  • Anteroinferior plating — easier trajectory, longer screws, lower hardware prominence. Preferred by many centers.
  • Superior plating — more familiar, direct approach to fracture
  • Intramedullary nail (TEN / locked devices) — for simple transverse/short oblique fractures. Smaller incision. High hardware removal rate with unlocked devices.
  • Lateral third: hook plate (Type IIB/3B), CC screw/suture, anatomic lateral plate
  • Timing: ideally within 2–3 weeks. Delayed fixation risks brachial plexus tethering.

Author's Preferred Approach (Rockwood & Green)

For displaced midshaft fractures (Robinson 2B) in active patients: precontoured plate fixation via anterior approach within 2 weeks. For simple pattern fractures: consider IM nailing with locked device if canal accommodates. For lateral Type IIB: hook plate or anatomic lateral plate. Most other fractures: nonoperative with sling and early mobilization, shared decision-making.

08 — Surgical Approach

Anterior Approach to the Clavicle

AO

Anterior / Anteroinferior Approach — Diaphysis

Midshaft and middle-third clavicle fractures. Suitable for both anterior and superior plate application.

Beach-chair semi-sitting. Small pad behind ipsilateral shoulder. Head turned contralaterally. Endotracheal tube taped to opposite side.

Oblique 8–10 cm incision just inferior to the clavicle, centered over the fracture site. May be curved for medial third fractures.

Supraclavicular nerves (C3/C4) crossing superficially — preserve to avoid chest wall numbness. Subclavian vessels and brachial plexus postero-inferiorly — avoid deep sharp dissection medially.

AO

Anterior approach exposure showing trapezius (posterior), sternocleidomastoid (medial), deltoid and pectoralis major (anterior/lateral), platysma, and supraclavicular sensory nerves crossing the operative field.

Dissection Steps

  1. 1

    Incise platysma transversely and tag with sutures for later anatomic repair.

  2. 2

    Identify and carefully preserve supraclavicular sensory nerve branches crossing the field.

  3. 3

    Incise clavipectoral fascia to expose the underlying clavicle. This reveals pectoralis major anteriorly and trapezius posteriorly.

  4. 4

    Minimal subperiosteal dissection at the fracture site — preserve soft tissue attachments to all bone fragments to support healing.

Closure Pearl — AO

Incorporate the cut periosteum into the clavipectoral fascia repair to reduce dehiscence. Ensure anatomic platysma repair to avoid skin contour deformity. Oppose skin with minimal tension for optimal scar cosmesis.

09 — Surgical Technique

Open Reduction & Plate Fixation

  1. 1

    Positioning: Beach-chair, pad behind shoulder, arm padded and strapped at side. Fluoroscopy ipsi- or contralaterally as preferred — not mandatory for plate fixation.

  2. 2

    Approach: Anterior approach as above. Expose fracture with minimal periosteal stripping. Identify all fragments — a vertical anterosuperior butterfly fragment often benefits from lag screw fixation with a minifragment screw.

  3. 3

    Reduction: Manual superior translation of the distal fragment (elevate the shoulder). Joystick or reduction clamps on fragments. Key maneuver: push shoulder upward to reduce lateral fragment to medial fragment, reversing the deforming forces.

  4. 4

    Provisional fixation: K-wire or reduction clamp across fracture. Restore length — check against contralateral clavicle length on fluoroscopy.

  5. 5

    Plate selection and contouring: Use precontoured anatomic plate. Even anatomic plates often require intraoperative adjustment for sex/racial variation in clavicle shape. Straight plates require extensive contouring — avoid.

  6. 6

    Plate application: Superior surface (standard) or anteroinferior surface. Minimum 3 bicortical screws each side. For superior plating, drill trajectory is superoinferior. For anteroinferior plating, AP trajectory — safer from neurovascular injury.

  7. 7

    Screw placement: The clavicle is widest AP in the middle third — anteroinferior screws can be longer. Avoid posterior cortex violation in medial half (subclavian vein within 1 cm). Never drill blindly inferiorly in the medial third.

  8. 8

    Assess reduction: Confirm length, rotation, and alignment clinically and radiographically. Check shoulder symmetry. Ensure no iatrogenic neurovascular compromise.

  9. 9

    Closure: Repair clavipectoral fascia over plate. Anatomic platysma repair. Subcuticular skin closure. Sling postoperatively.

⚠ Surgical Pitfalls

Delayed fixation (>3 weeks) risks brachial plexus tethering to clavicle undersurface — dissect carefully. Avoid inferior sharp dissection in medial third. Cerclage wire in isolation is inadequate — avoid. Inadequate plate length or screw number leads to early hardware failure, especially in larger patients.

Postoperative Protocol

Early Phase (0–6 weeks)

  • Sling for comfort — not immobilization
  • Pendulum exercises from week 1–2
  • Wound check at 2 weeks
  • Active non-weight-bearing ROM to horizontal from 2–4 weeks
  • Radiographs at 6 weeks

Late Phase (6 weeks+)

  • Strengthening exercises when callus visible
  • Return to full activity: 3–4 months
  • Functional outcome plateaus at 1 year
  • Hardware removal: only if symptomatic prominence (not routine)
  • IM nail: routine removal often recommended at 7–8 weeks
10 — Complications & Outcomes

Complications & Prognosis

Nonunion

14–24% after displaced midshaft treated nonoperatively. Higher with comminution, displacement, female sex, age. Requires secondary plating ± bone graft. Painful neuroma can complicate nonunion site.

Malunion

Not merely cosmetic — associated with shoulder strength deficits up to 30% on objective testing. Characteristic pain, weakness, and droopy shoulder. Corrective osteotomy for symptomatic cases.

Hardware Prominence

Most common complication of plate fixation (~30%). More frequent with superior plating. Symptomatic hardware is primary indication for removal — not routine. Anteroinferior plating reduces prominence.

Infection

Reported in ~4% of operative cases. Superficial infections managed with oral antibiotics. Deep infection requires hardware removal and debridement. Prophylactic antibiotics at induction.

Neurovascular Injury

Brachial plexus injury <1% but devastating. Risk highest with delayed fixation (brachial plexus tethering). Supraclavicular nerve numbness common (20–30%) — usually resolves. Subclavian vessel injury rare.

Refracture / Hardware Failure

Refracture after hardware removal: avoid <1 year post-fixation. Hardware failure with inadequate plate strength (avoid reconstruction plates in large patients). IM nail migration with unlocked devices.

Prognosis

Both operative and nonoperative treatment yield good functional scores (DASH 2–12, Constant 80–98) at 1 year. Functional recovery plateaus at 1 year — functional outcome at 12 months is the definitive endpoint. Operative treatment provides lower nonunion rate, faster return of function, and better cosmesis, but at the cost of ~4% infection and ~30% hardware prominence. Inform patients that up to 41–46% report residual symptoms despite union after nonoperative treatment.
