Fractures & Dislocations of the Talus

The second most common tarsal fracture — and among the least forgiving.
Rockwood & Green's Fractures in Adults, 10e · Ch. 66 (Sanders & Inculet) · AO Surgery Reference

The Injury in Brief

The talus mechanically links the ankle, hindfoot, and transverse tarsal joints. It has no muscular or tendinous attachments, is largely covered in cartilage, and depends on a tenuous, retrograde blood supply — which is why displaced fractures are dominated by osteonecrosis and post-traumatic arthritis.

Mechanism

  • Neck — high-energy axial load with the ankle dorsiflexed; the plafond drives into the neck (falls from height, MVCs). Peterson disproved pure hyperdorsiflexion; 46% of neck fractures follow an MVC.
  • Body — axial compression of an anteriorly subluxated talus (plafond onto the dome). In one series body fractures were the most common talar fracture (47%).
  • Head — compressive/shear force across the talonavicular joint with subluxation.
  • Lateral process ("snowboarder's fracture") — axial load, hindfoot eversion, ankle dorsiflexion.
  • Posterior process — axial compression in extreme plantarflexion, or PTFL avulsion in inversion; distinguish from an os trigonum.

Signs, symptoms & the soft-tissue exam

  • Younger, male-predominant population; swelling and pain. Women show a biphasic age distribution with more low-energy process fractures.
  • Inspect for open wounds and impending skin compromise — tenting from a displaced neck/body demands urgent reduction.
  • Neurovascular exam before and after any manipulation; posteromedial body displacement threatens the posterior tibial nerve/artery. Check ABIs if pulses asymmetric (<0.9 → CT angiogram).

Imaging

  • Radiographs — AP, lateral, mortise of the ankle plus AP/oblique foot. Mortise best shows the lateral process and dome; lateral shows dorsiflexion/varus neck angulation and subtalar/TN congruity.
  • Canale view — foot plantigrade, pronated 15°, beam 75° to perpendicular; assesses neck reduction and medial length.
  • Brodén view — best for the subtalar joint and retained fragments.
  • CT — routine for planning; MRI for occult/stress fractures and early osteonecrosis (edema by ~week 2 post-insult).
Detail — associated injuries & ATLS

High-energy mechanisms produce polytrauma: up to 44% of talus fractures presenting to a level-1 centre were polytrauma, with associated-injury rates as high as 89%. The most common ipsilateral fractures are the ankle (43%) and calcaneus (28%); internal-organ injury occurs in up to 20%. Evaluation follows ATLS with a disciplined tertiary survey — missed orthopaedic injuries remain common. Displaced fractures or joints not reduced closed become an operative priority (internal or external fixation) to protect the soft-tissue envelope.

Three Zones, One Bone

The talus divides into body, neck, and head — no tendon or muscle attaches to it, and most of its surface is articular cartilage, leaving little room for vascular ingrowth.

  • Body — most of the ankle (dome) and subtalar articulation. Wider anteriorly than posteriorly: with the foot plantarflexed this can falsely widen the mortise on radiograph.
  • Neck — deviates medially 15–20° from the body's long axis; superior surface largely non-cartilaginous with perforating vessels, and directly vulnerable to plafond impaction.
  • Head — entirely cartilaginous, constrained within the acetabulum pedis (navicular, anterior calcaneus, spring ligament, PTT insertion).

Processes & landmarks

  • Lateral process — broad wedge of the inferolateral body; articulates with fibula (dorsolaterally) and posterior calcaneal facet (inferomedially); origin of the lateral talocalcaneal ligament.
  • Posterior process — posteromedial + posterolateral tubercles with the FHL between them. The larger lateral tubercle may exist as an os trigonum (present in ~50% of feet).

Blood Supply — Why the Talus Fails

Vascular pearl
The artery of the tarsal canal (from the posterior tibial artery) supplies the lateral two-thirds of the body and is considered the most important source. The posterior tibial artery's deltoid branch may become the sole remaining supply after a displaced neck fracture — preserve it (and the deep deltoid) during medial malleolar osteotomy.
  • Posterior tibial artery → artery of tarsal canal (anastomotic sling with tarsal sinus a.) → inferior neck + posterior body; deltoid branch → medial body.
  • Anterior tibial / dorsalis pedis → dorsal neck and head; often injured by dorsal neck impaction.
  • Peroneal artery → posterolateral talus via anastomosis.
Detail — ligamentous attachments of the neck

The lateral neck gives rise to the inferior extensor retinaculum, interosseous talocalcaneal ligament, and the ATFL insertion. The medial neck carries strong deltoid and talonavicular fibres; the inferomedial neck articulates with the middle calcaneal facet. Anterior ankle capsular ligaments attach to the superior neck — severe plantarflexion can produce capsular avulsion fractures here.

Classifying Talar Fractures

Hawkins (1970) governs talar neck fractures and predicts osteonecrosis; the OTA/AO (81) compendium spans the whole bone by anatomic location and comminution.

Detail — how the two systems relate

Head (81-A3), neck (81-B2/B3), and body (81-C1/C2/C3) fractures are further graded by comminution, which independently predicts outcome in large neck-fracture series. The B-types map directly onto Hawkins I–III. Body fractures are shearing (sagittal or coronal) or compression type; they are distinguished from neck fractures by a dorsal fracture line running posterior to the lateral process and into the posterior facet.

Treatment Principles

Goals: timely anatomic reduction of the fracture and every peritalar joint, stable fixation, and early motion. Sangeorzan showed as little as 2 mm of neck displacement significantly alters subtalar contact.

Nonoperative — a narrow lane

  • Truly nondisplaced neck, body, head, or process fractures; incomplete/stress fractures; poor surgical candidates.
  • Only Hawkins I neck fractures qualify — confirm with CT; any displacement/subluxation reclassifies as Hawkins II and mandates surgery.
  • Process/avulsion fractures: 6 weeks non-weight-bearing, then protected weight-bearing and ROM.
Do not delay
Neck fractures with subtalar (Hawkins IIB) or ankle (Hawkins III) dislocation and any skin tenting require urgent reduction. Multiple failed closed attempts damage bone and soft tissue — proceed to open reduction (usually a posteromedial incision) rather than repeated forceful manipulation.

Detail — extruded talus & posterior process

Extruded talus is a salvage scenario. If soft-tissue attachments remain, reimplant urgently after I&D with external fixation/K-wire stabilization (largest series: 27 reimplanted, infection in only 2). A completely extruded, clean talus may be pouched intra-abdominally for a few days before reinsertion. If missing, grossly contaminated, or comminuted: antibiotic cement spacer + ex-fix, with delayed Masquelet reconstruction/fusion. Rarely, amputation.

Posterior process: small/nondisplaced → cast; fragments involving the posterior subtalar joint → ORIF to preserve stability and prevent rapid arthrosis.

  • Medial: accentuate inversion to unlock the calcaneus, then plantarflex + traction, evert. Blocks: extensor retinaculum, EDB, capsule, peroneals, TN impaction.

Surgical Approaches

Displaced neck fractures use a two-incision technique — medial and anterolateral — to judge rotation and alignment on both sides. Keep a 5–7 cm skin bridge and raise full-thickness flaps.

Medial (anteromedial)

  • Interval between anterior tibial and posterior tibial tendons; exposes superomedial body/neck and medial midfoot.
  • Performed first when there is no neck comminution; site more posteriorly if a medial malleolar osteotomy is planned.
  • Repair the incised talonavicular capsule to avoid medial instability.

Anterolateral

  • From the anterolateral ankle corner toward the fourth metatarsal; exposes neck, ankle, and (with care for sinus tarsi vessels) the subtalar joint.
  • Allows a lateral malleolar osteotomy without a separate incision when lateral body comminution exists.

Sinus tarsi

  • From the inferior fibular tip in line with the fourth ray; preferred for lateral-process/inferior-talus fractures and subtalar loose bodies.
  • Protect peroneals within their sheath; tag ATFL/CFL for repair.
Osteotomy pearl
A medial malleolar osteotomy improves posterior body exposure while preserving the deep deltoid ligament and its perforating artery — potentially the talus's last blood supply. Pre-drill screw holes before cutting; complete the articular cut with an osteotome to protect cartilage and allow interdigitated reduction.
Detail — alternatives to osteotomy

A posteromedial approach augmented with a joint distractor and gastrocnemius recession gives body exposure comparable to a medial malleolar osteotomy in cadaveric work, and offers greater dome exposure than a posterolateral approach. Positioning is supine with an ipsilateral hip bump; lateral decubitus (bean bag) is used for isolated lateral-process ORIF.

Complications & Salvage

Outcome tracks the initial injury: open wounds, dislocation, and comminution drive complications regardless of technique.

Hawkins sign
Subchondral lucency in the talar dome on the mortise view at 6–8 weeks indicates preserved vascularity and excludes osteonecrosis (highly specific). Its absence does not confirm AVN. MRI detects AVN earliest (~3 weeks) but does not change acute management.
  • The talus often heals despite AVN; prolonged non-weight-bearing beyond ~12 weeks is impractical and unproven. Collapse (on revascularization) — most often equinovarus — drives reconstruction, typically TTC fusion.

Malunion

  • Varus (medial neck comminution not appreciated) → short medial column, forefoot supination, lateral overload. Reported in 28–32% historically. Prevent with lateral plate for length + Canale view.
  • Dorsal → loss of declination, anterior impingement, dorsiflexion loss. Restore the natural declination angle; use contralateral imaging.

Nonunion, infection & arthritis

  • Nonunion/delayed union: rare (0–4%, avg 2.5%). Many radiographic 6-month nonunions still unite.
  • Infection/osteomyelitis: up to 41% postoperative infection across open talar fracture-dislocations; risk rises with Gustilo–Anderson grade, age, smoking. Avascular body acts as a sequestrum — aggressive debridement, antibiotic spacer, culture-directed IV antibiotics.
  • Post-traumatic arthritis: 46–97% after neck fractures. Diagnostic/therapeutic injections localise the joint; isolated fusion (ankle, subtalar, or TN) vs TTC for combined disease.
Salvage ladder
For end-stage AVN with collapse: TTC arthrodesis with bulk femoral-head allograft + hindfoot nail is the workhorse. Emerging options include porous-tantalum spacers, the modified Blair fusion (retains head/neck, sliding tibial graft), and third-generation total talus replacement — all with limited long-term data. Failure → TTC fusion or below-knee amputation.
Detail — secondary procedures & outcomes

Even after appropriate ORIF, Sanders et al. reported revision surgery in 25% of patients within 1 year and 50% within 10 years for severe neck fractures — most commonly subtalar/triple arthrodesis, then ankle arthrodesis, driven by post-traumatic arthritis (linked to comminution, Hawkins III/IV, and ipsilateral extremity injury). Subtalar dislocations generally do well when stable and closed, though stiffness is near-universal; open/high-energy dislocations show up to ~89% radiographic ankle/subtalar arthritis at 5 years and AVN up to 50%.
