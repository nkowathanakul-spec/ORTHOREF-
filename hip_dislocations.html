Hip Dislocations & Femoral Head Fractures

High-energy injuries of an inherently stable joint — mechanism, classification, emergent reduction, and the race against avascular necrosis.
Source: Rockwood & Green's Fractures in Adults, 10e — Ch. 52 (Kain & Tornetta) · OTA/AO 2018 Compendium

Overview & Mechanism

The hip is an inherently stable ball-and-socket joint that requires significant force to dislocate. Dislocations occur predominantly in younger patients from high-energy motor vehicle trauma, and a dislocation — with or without a femoral head fracture — is presumed to be a marker of major trauma until proven otherwise.

Board pearl

Posterior dislocations outnumber anterior 9:1. The position of the hip at axial loading determines the injury: more flexion/adduction/IR → pure dislocation; less of each → fracture-dislocation.

Mechanism & the position-of-impact concept

  • Posterior — deceleration / dashboard injury: knee strikes dashboard with hip & knee flexed. Letournel's vector analysis: more flexion + adduction → pure dislocation.
  • Anterior — hyperabduction + extension + external rotation (e.g., motorcycle). Degree of flexion sets the subtype: extension → superior/pubic; flexion → inferior/obturator.
  • Anteversion matters: decreased femoral anteversion (or retroversion) places the head posteriorly and favors pure dislocation; greater anteversion favors fracture-dislocation.
  • FAI morphology (CAM / PINCER) and femoral head fatigue/insufficiency fractures (military recruits, new exercise regimens) are additional contributors.

Associated injuries — assume a polytrauma patient

  • Up to 95% of patients have injuries requiring inpatient management independent of the dislocation. Intra-abdominal, head, and chest trauma are common.
  • Ipsilateral knee injuries in ~25% (dashboard): PCL injury, posterior knee dislocation, patellar fracture. Anterior knee bruise/laceration is a red flag.
  • Marymont described thoracic aortic injury association with posterior dislocations.
  • Occult femoral neck fracture is the most dangerous miss — high-quality neck films ± fine-cut CT before any closed reduction.
Do not miss

A nondisplaced femoral neck fracture must be ruled out (and stabilized with percutaneous screws) before reduction — closed manipulation can displace it iatrogenically.

Signs & symptoms

Posterior dislocation

  • Limb shortened, flexed, adducted, internally rotated
  • Any attempt to extend/externally rotate is exquisitely painful

Anterior dislocation

  • Limb externally rotated, variable flexion/abduction
  • Rare femoral vessel injury
  • Prereduction sciatic nerve exam is mandatory (the nerve can be injured during reduction). Test all branches including foot eversion — peroneal weakness may be the only finding.
  • Check distal pulses (associated posterior knee dislocation) and clear the spine/pelvis radiographically.

Biomechanics in brief

  • Acetabulum opens anteriorly & inferiorly; horseshoe articular cartilage is thickest laterally/peripherally (peripheral loading).
  • Femoral head ≈ two-thirds of a sphere. Normal anteversion ~12°, neck-shaft angle ~125°.
  • Joint reaction force: ~⅓ body weight in double-leg stance, up to 6× body weight in gait, >2× body weight just using a bedpan. Toe-touch/foot-flat WB generates the least force.

Surgical & Applied Anatomy

Quick reference

Capsular ligaments

  • Iliofemoral (Y) ligament of Bigelow — strongest; AIIS → intertrochanteric line in two bands. Usually intact in posterior dislocation.
  • Pubofemoral — inferior support, deep to the Y ligament.
  • Ischiofemoral — posterior, within the capsule; short external rotators add support.
  • Labrum adds >10% coverage (head stays >50% covered); >400 N to distract.

Sciatic nerve (Beaton)

  • ~84% single trunk anterior/deep to piriformis · ~12% peroneal pierces · ~3% surrounds · ~1% through.
  • Peroneal division most often injured — test foot eversion; prereduction palsy up to 19%.

Blood supply

  • MFCA dominant (retinacular/cervical branches); protect in posterior approaches.
  • Foveal artery (obturator a., in ligamentum teres) feeds the epiphyseal head in ~75%.

Imaging & Diagnostic Studies

The trauma AP pelvis is the single most useful first study — the diagnosis should be apparent on this one view. The key sign is loss of congruence of the femoral head with the acetabular roof (sourcil).

AP pelvis signs

  • Posterior dislocation → head appears smaller and overlaps the roof; femur internally rotated (lesser trochanter hidden, neck in profile).
  • Anterior dislocation → head appears larger, medial or inferior to the acetabulum.
  • Inspect carefully for occult neck fracture, head fragment, acetabular/pelvic injury before attempting reduction.

Post-reduction series & CT

  • After reduction obtain 5 views: AP, both Judet (45° oblique), inlet, outlet — centered on the pelvis to compare with the normal hip.
  • First question on every view: is the reduction concentric? Compare the head–sourcil relationship to the contralateral side; it must be symmetric.
  • Fine-cut CT is more sensitive for small intra-articular fragments, head/impaction fractures, and incongruity. The head should sit centered in the subchondral ring — a "bullseye." A 0.5 mm asymmetry can indicate subluxation.
MRI / SPECT

MRI is best for soft tissue (labrum, short external rotators, cartilage) and insufficiency/impaction injuries; AVN changes may not appear for 6–8 weeks. In children/adolescents MRI beats CT for entrapped labrum and unossified posterior wall. SPECT distinguishes avascular from impaction changes but does not reliably predict AVN peri-injury.

Management

The initial management for almost all dislocations is an emergent attempt at closed reduction to get the head under the sourcil — ideally within 6 hours. Contraindications: a (possibly nondisplaced) femoral neck fracture and injuries preventing use of the limb to manipulate the hip.

Step Walkthrough — Closed Reduction

Nonoperative treatment

Indications

  • Pure dislocation, congruent & stable after reduction
  • Congruent joint ± small fragment in the cotyloid fossa
  • Pipkin I or small type II with anatomic reduction
  • No mechanical block to motion

Contraindications

  • Pipkin III & IV
  • Incongruent reduction
  • Loose bodies interfering with articulation
  • Femoral neck fracture (displaced or nondisplaced)
Time is cartilage — and blood supply

Reduction within 6 hours yields AVN rates of 0–10%. Delay beyond 12 hours increases AVN risk 5.6×. No more than two closed attempts.

Fluoroscopic stress examination

For a congruent hip with a small posterior wall or Pipkin I fracture that doesn't mandate fixation by size, an exam-under-anesthesia stress test determines stability: under fluoroscopy the flexed/internally rotated hip is stressed in line with the femur (AP, obturator oblique, obturator outlet views). Any loss of the congruent head–roof relationship = posterior subluxation = unstable → fix.

Why stability matters

Hougaard: 83% of unstable posterior-wall hips treated nonoperatively developed arthritis. Olson: a posterior wall defect as small as 27% alters joint contact forces. Fix any wall fracture that allows instability, regardless of size.

Operative treatment

Indications

  • Irreducible dislocation (2–15% of dislocations) — emergent open reduction via the direction of dislocation
  • Incongruent reduction from incarcerated fragment or soft-tissue interposition
  • Iatrogenic sciatic nerve injury after attempted reduction
  • Pipkin II/III/IV requiring fixation; unstable posterior wall

Causes of irreducibility — Anterior

  • Buttonholing through capsule
  • Rectus femoris, psoas tendon
  • Labrum, capsule

Causes of irreducibility — Posterior

  • Piriformis, obturator internus, gluteus maximus
  • Capsule, ligamentum teres, labrum
  • Incarcerated bony fragment / posterior wall

ORIF principles — femoral head fractures

  • Pipkin I (infrafoveal): excise or debride if it blocks motion — often via anterior approach (fragment is anteromedial).
  • Pipkin II (suprafoveal, weight-bearing): anatomic reduction; recessed headless / low-profile compression screws in subchondral bone. Hardware must never protrude.
  • Fracture line typically 25–45° off the sagittal plane, fragment anteromedial (Moed & Maxey).
  • Large impaction (>2 cm² of weight-bearing surface) → elevate & graft.
Approach & AVN

The anterior approach directly visualizes the anteromedial fragment without redislocation and does not increase AVN vs posterior (Swiontkowski) — but has higher HO (~40%). Avoid the posterior approach for head fixation when possible (further compromises the MFCA).

Hip arthroscopy

  • Relative indication: removal of small loose bodies in a stable hip with non-concentric or persistently symptomatic reduction.
  • Mullis: loose bodies found in 78% of hips lacking radiographic evidence of them.
  • Caution with acetabular fractures — fluid extravasation into the pelvis; prefer open. Use postless traction to protect the pudendal nerve.

Authors' preferred algorithm (52-1)

Decision flow

Goal: a congruent, stable hip with minimal head ischemia.

  • Associated neck fracture → percutaneous screws (or open reduction if displaced) before/with hip reduction.
  • Irreducible → immediate open reduction via direction of dislocation.
  • Then triage to one of three: congruent without fracture · congruent with fracture · incongruent.
  • Incongruent → open reduction + remove offending structure. Congruent + small caudal (I) → nonop or excise. Congruent + weight-bearing (II), not anatomic → ORIF. Pipkin III/IV → surgical dislocation.

Post-operative care highlights

  • Posterior precautions for posterior injuries: limit flexion to 60–90° and IR; opposite (limit ER/extension) for anterior.
  • Foot-flat partial WB after pure dislocations & Pipkin fixation; Pipkin III/IV full WB delayed to 12 weeks.
  • VTE prophylaxis (LMWH / factor Xa, given the energy); HO prophylaxis after open procedures (indomethacin 75 mg/day × 3 wk, or single-dose radiation in highest-risk).

Surgical Approaches

Direction of dislocation and fragment location dictate the approach: irreducible dislocations are approached from the direction of the dislocation. For head fractures, anterior exposure has become favored.

Anterior — Smith-Petersen / Hueter (DAA) & Watson-Jones

  • Direct visualization of the anteromedial head fragment without redislocating the hip; external rotation exposes the fracture bed.
  • Watch the LFCN: keep the incision lateral (posterior border of TFL) and sweep tissue lateral→medial; warn patients about thigh numbness.
  • Ascending branch of the lateral femoral circumflex vessels in the deep fascia between sartorius and TFL — identify and ligate/clip/cauterize; a constant oozing source.
  • Limited medial exposure → release rectus from AIIS or osteotomize the AIIS (± ASIS) for more medial/inferior access.

Posterior — Kocher–Langenbeck

  • Used for posterior wall fixation; limited anterior visualization of the joint.
  • Protect the MFCA: release piriformis & external rotators through their tendinous insertions medially, leaving a femoral-side sleeve; stay along the acetabular rim posteriorly.
  • Be prepared to convert to a trochanteric osteotomy / surgical dislocation.

Surgical dislocation (Ganz)

  • Preferred for Pipkin III & IV and large intra-articular fragments — complete 360° visualization of head and acetabulum.
  • Trochanteric osteotomy from the tip to just distal to the vastus ridge, anterior to gluteus maximus insertion; the muscles/tendons are not cut.
  • Z-shaped capsulotomy and osteotomy through the trochanteric tip protect the MFCA. Preserve the ligament of Weitbrecht — it carries blood supply to the Pipkin fragment.
Outcome signal

Henle: surgical dislocation gave 83% good-to-excellent vs 56% for other approaches. Pipkin IV via surgical dislocation: 21 of 24 good/excellent at 2 years across two series.

Complications

AVN and post-traumatic arthritis are the two outcome-defining complications. Both correlate with injury severity, fracture-dislocation pattern, and — critically — time to reduction.

Avascular necrosis (AVN)

  • Reported in 1.7–40%; predominantly after posterior dislocation. Reduction within 6 h → 0–10%; delay >12 h → 5.6× risk.
  • Posterior approach carries 2–3× the AVN risk of an anterior approach or trochanteric osteotomy.
  • Mechanism is largely ischemia from kinking/spasm while dislocated (not necessarily torn vessels) — early reduction restores flow.
  • Radiographic changes usually by 2 years (up to 5). Post-traumatic AVN may be localized and amenable to osteotomy.

Post-traumatic arthritis

  • Most common complication after dislocation; more common posteriorly and in fracture-dislocations (up to 50% with head fractures).
  • Driven by chondrocyte death at impact (20–30% strain induces apoptosis) and incongruity / third-body wear.
  • Prolonged dislocation time increases chondrocyte apoptosis (animal models) — another argument for speed.

Heterotopic ossification

  • Common after posterior fracture-dislocation and after open reduction; higher after anterior approaches for head fractures (~40–58%).
  • Prophylaxis: indomethacin; radiation reserved for highest risk (carcinoma concern limits routine use).

Sciatic nerve dysfunction

  • Prereduction palsy up to 19%; peroneal division most affected. Functional recovery in ~70% (partial palsies do better than complete).
  • If function worsens after reduction → explore (nerve may be entrapped). Late dysfunction is usually from HO — examine the nerve at every visit.
  • Routine acute exploration is not recommended; tendon transfer / AFO if no recovery.
