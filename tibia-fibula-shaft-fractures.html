Tibia & Fibula Shaft FracturesThe most frequently fractured long bone — where outcome is governed by the soft-tissue envelope.

Perhaps more so than for any other bone, the treatment and outcome of tibia fractures are governed primarily by the extent of the associated soft-tissue injury — not by the fracture morphology alone.

Epidemiology & Mechanism
  • Historically the most frequently fractured long bone; the diaphysis is the most common tibial fracture site.
  • Bimodal distribution: low-energy spiral patterns predominate in patients >50 yr; high-energy transverse/comminuted patterns in patients <30 yr.
  • Low-energy causes: falls from standing height and sport (soccer, skiing). High-energy: vehicular trauma — pedestrians struck are the single most common mechanism.
  • Because the anteromedial surface is subcutaneous, open fractures are common and, when open, are more often type IIIB (requiring flap coverage) than at other sites.

Associated Injuries

Compartment syndrome

Occurs in 1.5–11%. High index of suspicion is mandatory — missed cases are devastating and a leading source of litigation.

Ankle / posterior malleolus

Plafond extension in 8–9% of shaft fractures but 25–69% of distal-third spiral patterns — usually a Haraguchi type I posterior malleolar fragment. Consider preoperative CT.

Floating knee

Ipsilateral femoral + tibial fracture. Higher rates of vascular injury (21%) and open fracture (62%); good results achievable when both are nailed.

Plateau extension

Occult proximal extension in proximal-third fractures. A central nondisplaced split can displace during nailing — clamp/screw or lateral plate first. Consider preoperative CT of the knee.

Proximal tibiofibular joint dislocation

~1.5% of shaft fractures (usually with an intact fibula). Marker of severity: compartment syndrome 29%, open 63%, peroneal nerve injury 36%.

Knee ligament injury

Underdiagnosed acutely; MCL sprain seen in ~22% under anaesthesia but few require surgery.

Assessment — skin & neurovascular

Skin & soft tissue

  • Relieve tenting/puckering by restoring alignment — full-thickness necrosis can occur within hours.
  • Assume any wound on a fractured limb is an open fracture until proven otherwise; lateral/posterolateral wounds often overlie the fibula fracture at a different level.
  • Post-splint radiographs to confirm the displacing deformity is corrected.

Neurovascular

  • Displaced fractures may kink vessels — recheck pulses after reduction; persistent deficit → angiography/CTA/Doppler.
  • Document motor grade specifically (e.g. "tibialis anterior 3/5, limited by pain").
  • Test sensation in deep peroneal, superficial peroneal, sural, saphenous, and tibial territories.
ImagingFull-length AP + lateral centred on the mid-tibia, plus dedicated knee and ankle views. Because of ~20° of normal tibial torsion, a standard AP of the knee and ankle cannot be obtained on one film. A lipohaemarthrosis suggests occult articular injury. CT of the ankle for distal-third spiral patterns; CT of the knee for proximal-third patterns.

A triangular subcutaneous diaphysis carrying >80% of axial load, bound to the fibula by the interosseous membrane, and wrapped by four tight fascial compartments.

Osteology

Key points
  • The tibial diaphysis is triangular in cross-section with thick cortices; cortical thickness decreases with age, predisposing older patients to low-energy injury.
  • The tibia carries >80% of axial load; the fibula bears only 7–16% of body weight. The two bones are linked along their length by the interosseous membrane.
  • That membranous connection explains why so many tibial fractures have an associated fibula fracture — and why fixing the tibia usually restores enough stability for the fibula to heal.
  • Fractures at or near the same level in both bones are intrinsically less stable.
  • The distal articular surface is externally rotated ~20° relative to the proximal surface (tibial torsion). The mechanical axis runs from femoral head centre to the centre of the ankle, passing just medial to the knee centre.

Vascularity

Key points
  • Dual supply: medullary (endosteal) and periosteal. Recent evidence attributes ~91% of cortical blood supply to the medullary system.
  • The dominant nutrient artery is a branch of the posterior tibial artery, entering the bone in its proximal third.
  • Periosteal supply derives from surrounding soft tissue and is disrupted by stripping at injury or surgery — after stripping, outer-cortex viability depends on centrifugal flow from the medullary system.
  • Reaming temporarily reduces medullary flow, but the periosteal system compensates; clinically, the feared harm of reaming open fractures has not borne out.
Why it mattersPreserving soft-tissue attachments — and therefore periosteal blood supply — is the single most important surgical principle when plating the tibia. Excessive stripping devitalises bone and drives infection and nonunion.

Compartments & Musculature

The leg has four compartments with remarkably little person-to-person variation. Their contents are the anatomical basis for fasciotomy, motor/sensory examination, and surgical approach planning.

Anterior & lateral compartment — surgical relevance

Anterior compartment

Dorsiflexors of ankle and toes. Its muscles originate from the anterolateral proximal tibia and are dissected off and retracted laterally during the anterolateral approach for plating.

Lateral compartment

Everters of the foot. The superficial peroneal nerve pierces the fascia ~10–12 cm proximal to the distal fibular tip — at risk during lateral fasciotomy, distal fibular fixation, and percutaneous distal plating.

Compartment syndrome — key figuresOccurs in 1.5–11% of tibia fractures. Diagnostic threshold: perfusion pressure (diastolic BP − compartment pressure) <30 mm Hg. Pulselessness is unusual and implies either a vascular injury or a pressure high enough to occlude arterial flow.

Fracture morphology is classified by the OTA/AO 42 system — but in clinical practice it is "relatively meaningless without a classification of the associated soft-tissue injury."

Predictive valueThe OTA/AO grade predicts acute compartment syndrome risk but is a poor predictor of functional outcome. Soft-tissue grade drives treatment and prognosis — an open fracture of any grade increases the risk of infection, nonunion, malunion, and reoperation.
Other systems worth knowing — OTA-OFC & MESS

OTA-OFC (open fracture)

Grades five features 1–3 — skin, muscle, arterial, contamination, bone loss. Better predictor of short-term outcome than Gustilo; a summative score >10 may signal low salvage potential.

MESS (mangled extremity)

Scores 2–11 on energy, ischaemia, shock, and age (ischaemia >6 h is doubled). A score >7 was the classic amputation threshold, but modern data show high scores are not reliable predictors of amputation — no longer used to mandate amputation.

Operative fixation — most often intramedullary nailing — is now preferred for most displaced tibial shaft fractures, but a defined role for functional bracing remains.

Nonoperative Treatment

Relative indications

  • Adequate alignment/length/rotation achievable in a cast
  • Soft tissues tolerate a cast
  • Significant anaesthetic risk
  • Patient declines surgery

Relative contraindications

  • Inadequate reduction after splint/cast
  • Open fracture · arterial injury
  • Displaced proximal or distal fracture
  • Compartment syndrome (or high risk)
  • Soft tissues won't tolerate a cast/brace
  • Ipsilateral injury preventing weight-bearing

Technique progresses from a well-padded (bivalved) long-leg cast for 2–4 weeks to a short-leg patellar-tendon-bearing cast or a functional brace (which also permits ankle motion). Bracing works via hydraulic pressure on the non-compressible soft tissues, so a tight, well-moulded fit and early weight-bearing are essential; malalignment is corrected by cast wedging.

Unlike the humerus, tibial alignment does not improve during nonoperative treatment.

Operative Treatment — Intramedullary Nailing

IM nailing is the most common procedure and is favoured over closed treatment for nonunion, malunion, complications, and return to work. Reaming permits a larger nail and promotes healing; the large SPRINT trial showed reaming is safe even in open fractures. Most North American surgeons ream all nails and avoid a tourniquet during reaming (thermal-necrosis concern).

Key surgical sequence — step by step
  1. Position supine (bump under ipsilateral hip); imaging from knee to ankle.
  2. Establish the start point: just medial to the lateral tibial spine (AP) and in line with the anterior cortex (lateral), between the joint line and tubercle.
  3. Entry reamer over the starting wire → pass a ball-tipped guidewire across the reduced fracture to center–center at the plafond (optimal endpoint: lateral-to-center on AP, centered/slightly posterior on lateral).
  4. Ream to ~1.5 mm greater than nail diameter, keeping the fracture reduced to avoid eccentric reaming.
  5. Pass the nail; lock distally then proximally. "Back-slap" for compression in axially stable patterns. Avoid distraction (nonunion risk).
  6. Confirm every interlock passes through the nail; check rotation clinically and fluoroscopically.

Infrapatellar vs Suprapatellar

Both are effective. Suprapatellar (semi-extended) may reduce malalignment and fluoroscopy time and eases proximal/distal reduction; concerns about patellofemoral cartilage and septic arthritis have largely not borne out. Infrapatellar remains the traditional workhorse; needs adequate knee flexion.

Blocking ("Poller") screws

Placed where you want the nail not to be — typically the concave side of the deformity — to funnel the reamer/nail into the correct path in wide metaphyseal segments. May be left in for added stability.

Distal A–P interlocksPercutaneous anterior-to-posterior distal locking endangers the anterior tendons (tibialis anterior, EHL) and the anterior neurovascular bundle (anterior tibial artery, deep peroneal nerve). Use a mini-open approach to define and protect the anatomy.
Proximal & distal malalignment — prevention
  • Proximal fractures classically deform into valgus + apex anterior (procurvatum): a too-medial start point or reamer creep causes valgus; a too-posterior trajectory plus extensor pull causes procurvatum. Correct start point/angle, reduce before reaming, and consider suprapatellar nailing, blocking screws, percutaneous clamps, temporary ex-fix ("traveling traction"), or provisional plating.
  • Distal fractures: the wide metaphysis lets the nail wander. Aim the guidewire endpoint lateral-to-center (AP) and centered (lateral); blocking screws and clamps help. Plating a coexisting distal fibula fracture first can restore length/alignment (though fibular fixation remains controversial and one report noted higher nonunion risk).
Plate Fixation
  • Less common than nailing; useful when nailing is precluded — periprosthetic TKA, a canal too small/deformed, or an ipsilateral plateau fracture.
  • Definitive plating of open fractures is controversial (deep infection ~11%); an alternative is temporary ex-fix or provisional plating followed by delayed definitive fixation once soft tissues settle.
  • Modern technique is minimally invasive/percutaneous (MIPO) with indirect reduction to preserve blood supply; simple patterns get direct reduction + lag screw + neutralisation plate.
External Fixation
  • Chiefly a temporising tool — damage control, or to let swelling/blisters resolve before plating/nailing.
  • Modern ring fixators allow gradual correction, weight-bearing, and distraction osteogenesis for bone-loss defects; a recent RCT found no deep-infection difference vs nailing for high-grade open fractures but more overall complications with rings.
  • Definitive external fixation carries high pin-tract infection rates. Many surgeons will not nail a tibia that has been in a frame for ≥2 weeks without a "pin holiday."
Authors' Preferred — Algorithm 63-1

Treatment is dictated by soft-tissue severity and fracture location. Closed and low-grade open injuries: nail (midshaft) or nail/plate per surgeon preference for proximal/distal patterns, with a suprapatellar start considered for proximal or distal fractures. Severe open injuries: repeated debridement, flap coverage, and — as a viable definitive option — ring external fixation.

The workhorse portal is the infrapatellar start for nailing; plating relies on the anterolateral, anteromedial, and posterolateral corridors — each a trade-off between access and soft-tissue cover.

Plating Corridors

Anterolateral Approach

Plate osteosynthesis · anterior compartment elevated off tibia
Technique notes
  • Incision just lateral to the tibial crest; the anterior tibial (tibialis anterior) muscle is elevated off the anterolateral surface of the tibia and retracted laterally.
  • Provides better soft-tissue cover for the plate — a lateral wound breakdown may be salvaged with a skin graft rather than a flap.
  • Ideal for percutaneous/submuscular plate sliding; the plate is passed through small incisions with stab incisions for screws.
At riskThe superficial peroneal nerve crosses the field distally (~10–12 cm above the fibular tip) — a larger incision is needed once distal percutaneous screws approach this level.

Anteromedial (Medial) Approach

Direct subcutaneous exposure · safe half-pin corridor
Technique notes
  • Directly exposes the subcutaneous medial tibia with no muscle to divide — the only structure at risk is the saphenous nerve and vein distally.
  • Commonly used for distal fractures via a limited or gently curved incision. The anteromedial surface is the safe corridor for unilateral external-fixator half-pins along the entire length of the bone.
  • Drawback: no deep soft-tissue cover — if the wound breaks down, hardware is exposed, so a curvilinear incision offset from the plate is often used.
TipStay superficial to the anterior-compartment fascia and avoid breaching the tibialis anterior tendon sheath distally to prevent adhesions.

Posterolateral Approach

Prone · interval: peroneals / FHL
Technique notes
  • Performed prone (or lateral), using the interval between the peroneal muscles (lateral compartment) and flexor hallucis longus (deep posterior compartment).
  • Excellent exposure of the distal three-fourths of the tibia with a robust muscular soft-tissue envelope; allows fibular plating through the same incision — useful for delayed plating and bone grafting.
  • Proximal exposure is limited by the takeoff of the anterior tibial artery; not suitable for proximal fractures and does not permit percutaneous plate insertion.

The tibia's slow, soft-tissue-dependent healing makes compartment syndrome, nonunion, malunion, and deep infection its defining complications.

Compartment Syndrome

  • Rate 1.5–11%. Independent risk factors: tibia fracture, male, younger age, proximal location, longer fracture patterns, and low-energy athletic mechanisms; ballistic proximal-third fractures are especially high-risk.
  • Nailing does not increase the risk. Diagnostic threshold: perfusion pressure <30 mm Hg (diastolic − compartment pressure).
  • With timely fasciotomy, compartment syndrome is not clearly associated with increased nonunion/infection.

Anterior Knee Pain

A poor outcome more than a complicationReported in 19–73% after IM nailing — multifactorial and poorly understood. Current evidence shows no reproducible difference between infrapatellar and suprapatellar techniques, and implant removal gives inconsistent relief. Discuss the risk before nailing.
Malunion
  • No agreed definition. Published "acceptable" limits: varus/valgus <5–10°, recurvatum/procurvatum <5–10°, rotation 0–10°, shortening <1–2 cm.
  • Cadaveric data: angulation can raise knee cartilage contact pressure by up to 106%, worst for proximal-third deformity. Yet several long-term series show no clear correlation between malunion and subsequent arthritis or function.
  • Symptomatic malunion is treated by osteotomy; multiplanar circular frames suit multiplanar correction and simultaneous lengthening.

Most common nonunion threshold
  • Risk rises with open fracture, deep infection, a postoperative fracture gap, distal location, and smoking.
  • The NURD score weights flap need, compartment syndrome, open fracture, male sex, ASA, cortical contact, and comorbidities: a score 0–5 → ~2% nonunion; >12 → ~61%.
  • Aseptic nailed nonunions are effectively treated with exchange nailing. Intervening before 6 months risks unnecessary surgery.
Deep Infection
  • Rate tracks fracture severity: 1.8% in closed/type I open, rising to 8–16% in type III injuries.
  • Most common organism is Staphylococcus aureus (~64% of deep tibial infections); nosocomial pathogens are increasingly reported.
  • Treatment may retain or remove the nail depending on healing stage; antibiotic nails add stability over beads/spacers. Osteomyelitis often needs aggressive debridement, saucerisation, and prolonged IV antibiotics; recalcitrant cases may end in amputation.
Symptomatic Hardware

Implant removal rates vary widely (3.5–65%) and are similar for plates and nails. Removing only the interlocking screws (often symptomatic when too long) carries a very low refracture risk; full nail/plate removal is delayed until well after union (authors wait ~1 year). Refracture after plate removal occurs in up to 11%. Pain resolves completely in only ~50% after removal.

Isolated Fibula Shaft Fractures & Maisonneuve

Almost always nonoperative

The intact tibia maintains length, rotation, and weight-bearing, so treatment is immediate weight-bearing as tolerated in a walking boot with a very high union rate. The central fibular diaphysis can even be harvested as a vascularised graft without significant functional loss.

Maisonneuve — the exception

An apparently isolated proximal spiral fibula fracture can signal a syndesmotic + interosseous membrane injury with medial malleolar/ligamentous disruption — highly unstable and usually needing syndesmotic and medial fixation. Always image the ipsilateral ankle (AP, lateral, mortise ± stress views).

Don't missBallistic fibula fractures — especially proximal-third — carry higher-than-expected compartment syndrome rates (~11.6%). Every apparent isolated fibula fracture warrants dedicated ankle imaging.
