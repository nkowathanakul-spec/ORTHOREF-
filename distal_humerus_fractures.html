Distal Humerus Fractures

Rockwood & Green, 10th Ed. · AO Foundation · Chapter 39

Overview

Epidemiology
  • Account for ~2% of all fractures and ~30% of distal humerus fractures in adults
  • Bimodal distribution: young high-energy trauma and elderly low-energy falls
  • Female predominance in elderly (osteoporotic bone)
  • Associated elbow dislocation in ~10–15%
R&G 10th Ed., Ch. 39, p. 1418
Mechanism
  • High-energy: axial load on flexed elbow (MVA, fall from height)
  • Low-energy: fall on outstretched hand in osteoporotic elderly
  • Wedge-like intrusion of olecranon into trochlea produces characteristic Y or T pattern
  • Direct posterior blow to flexed elbow
R&G 10th Ed., Ch. 39, p. 1419
⚠ Clinical Alert
Neurovascular assessment is mandatory. The ulnar nerve is most commonly injured (neuropraxia in 10–15%). Check radial pulse and median/radial/ulnar nerve function before and after reduction.
R&G 10th Ed., Ch. 39, p. 1428

Clinical Evaluation

History

  • Mechanism & energy
  • Dominant hand
  • Pre-injury elbow function
  • Osteoporosis / bone quality
  • Comorbidities (DM, RA)

Physical Exam

  • Swelling, ecchymosis, deformity
  • Open wound / skin integrity
  • Neurovascular: radial pulse, 3-nerve motor/sensory
  • Compartment assessment
  • Ipsilateral shoulder/wrist

Imaging

  • AP & Lateral X-rays (standard)
  • Traction X-rays — useful for comminuted fractures to assess articular fragments
  • CT with 3D reconstruction — gold standard for preoperative planning (AO recommendation)
  • CT arthrography rarely needed
AO Foundation; R&G 10th Ed., p. 1429

Surgical Anatomy

Osseous Architecture

The distal humerus widens into two diverging columns — medial and lateral — enclosing the olecranon and coronoid fossae. The articular surface consists of the trochlea medially (ulnohumeral joint) and the capitellum laterally (radiocapitellar joint). The columns and the intervening articular surface form a triangular construct that is the biomechanical basis of fixation strategy.

Coronal Plane
  • Valgus angle: 4–8°
  • Medial epicondyle: on shaft axis
  • Lateral epicondyle: distal to medial
Sagittal Plane
  • Anterior angulation: 35–40°
  • Both medial and lateral columns
  • Capitellum follows trochlea into flexion
Axial Plane
  • Internal rotation: 3–8°
  • Entire articular surface rotated
  • Must restore for full ROM
R&G 10th Ed., Ch. 39, Fig. 39-8, p. 1420

Ligamentous Stabilizers

Lateral Collateral Complex

  • Radial collateral ligament — isometric point on lateral epicondyle → annular ligament
  • Lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) — lateral epicondyle → crista supinatoris of proximal ulna; primary restraint to posterolateral rotatory instability
  • Annular ligament — anterior to posterior margin of lesser sigmoid notch
R&G 10th Ed., Fig. 39-11, p. 1422

Medial Collateral Complex

  • Anterior bundle (MCL) — anteroinferior medial epicondyle → sublime tubercle; prime stabilizer to valgus stress
  • Posterior bundle — posterior medial epicondyle; taut in flexion
  • Transverse ligament — connects anterior & posterior bundles; minimal role in stability
R&G 10th Ed., Fig. 39-12, p. 1423

Neurovascular Anatomy

Ulnar Nerve
  • Passes through cubital tunnel posterior to medial epicondyle
  • Most at-risk nerve in distal humerus surgery
  • Routine anterior transposition debated; protect during ORIF
Radial Nerve / PIN
  • Anterior interosseous branch at risk with lateral approach
  • Posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) winds around radial neck
  • Identify & protect during lateral column fixation
Vascular
  • Brachial artery bifurcates just distal to elbow
  • Rich collateral network via radial/ulnar recurrent arteries
  • Vascular injury uncommon but limb-threatening if missed
R&G 10th Ed., Fig. 39-13, p. 1424

AO/OTA Classification (13)

Type A — Extra-articular

  • A1: Apophyseal avulsion (epicondyle)
  • A2: Metaphyseal simple
  • A3: Metaphyseal multifragmentary
AO Foundation; R&G 10th Ed., p. 1430

Type B — Partial Articular

  • B1: Lateral sagittal (capitellum/lateral trochlea)
  • B2: Medial sagittal (medial trochlea)
  • B3: Frontal (coronal shear — Hahn-Steinthal/Kocher-Lorenz)
AO Foundation; R&G 10th Ed., p. 1430

Type C — Complete Articular

  • C1: Articular simple, metaphyseal simple (T/Y pattern)
  • C2: Articular simple, metaphyseal multifragmentary
  • C3: Articular multifragmentary (most complex)
AO Foundation; R&G 10th Ed., p. 1430

Treatment Principles

AO Principle
Goals: Anatomic articular reduction → Stable fixation → Early mobilization. The "parallel plating" (orthogonal or parallel plate constructs) of medial and lateral columns is the AO standard for bicolumn fractures.
AO Foundation; R&G 10th Ed., p. 1435

Non-operative Indications

  • Non-displaced or minimally displaced fractures (<2 mm displacement)
  • Patients medically unfit for surgery
  • Extra-articular A1 avulsions (small epicondyle fragment, no instability)
  • "Bag of bones" technique for severely comminuted fractures in elderly (historically used, now largely replaced by TEA)
R&G 10th Ed., Ch. 39, p. 1433

Operative Indications

  • Displaced intra-articular fractures — virtually all
  • Open fractures
  • Fractures with neurovascular compromise
  • Articular step-off >2 mm
  • Coronal plane shear fractures (B3)

Fixation Options

ORIF — Parallel Plate Technique

  • Medial and lateral column plates placed in parallel (both on posterior aspect)
  • Allows use of longer screws traversing articular segment
  • AO preferred construct for C1–C2 fractures
  • Achieve: articular reconstruction first → column fixation
  • Minimum 3 distal screws per column engaging opposite column
AO Foundation; R&G 10th Ed., p. 1440

ORIF — Orthogonal (90-90) Plate Technique

  • Medial plate on medial column; lateral plate on posterior lateral column at 90°
  • Traditional technique; equivalent biomechanical results
  • Medial plate directly on bone — less soft tissue stripping
  • Lateral plate may need contouring around posterior fossa
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1439; AO Foundation

Total Elbow Arthroplasty (TEA)

  • Indicated: elderly (>65 years), low-demand, C3 fracture with severe comminution, poor bone quality
  • Semi-constrained linked prosthesis (Coonrad-Morrey)
  • Permanent activity restriction: <5 kg repetitive, <1 kg single lift
  • Superior outcomes vs ORIF in RCTs for elderly C3
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1450; AO Foundation

Special Situations

  • Coronal shear (B3): headless compression screws (anterograde or retrograde); Herbert-Whipple, Acutrak
  • Lateral condyle isolated: lateral column plate ± lag screw
  • Epicondyle avulsion: screw fixation or suture anchor if small fragment
  • Open fracture: thorough debridement → provisional stabilization (ex-fix or definitive if clean)
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1445; AO Foundation

Step-by-Step ORIF Walkthrough

Surgical Approaches

All approaches use a posterior midline skin incision curving around the olecranon. The choice of deep approach depends on fracture type, need for visualization, and surgeon preference.

1. Olecranon Osteotomy (Gold Standard for Complex Articular)

  • Indication: C-type bicolumnar fractures requiring maximal articular visualization
  • Technique: Transverse or Chevron osteotomy at the bare area (apex of trochlear notch, 20 mm from tip)
  • Chevron preferred — rotational stability, larger surface area
  • Pre-drill for tension band wire or plate fixation before osteotomy
  • Triceps reflected with olecranon proximally — full 270° visualization of articular surface
  • Fixation: Tension band wiring (two K-wires + figure-of-8 wire) or cancellous screw + washer
  • Risk: Non-union (2–5%); painful hardware requiring removal
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1436; AO Foundation

2. Paratricipital (Bryan-Morrey) Approach

  • Indication: Extra-articular (A-type) and selected simple articular fractures; TEA
  • Triceps insertion left intact — windows on each side of tendon
  • Medial window: identify and mobilize ulnar nerve; reflects medial head of triceps
  • Lateral window: between triceps and anconeus
  • Limited articular visualization — NOT suitable for complex C-type fractures
  • Lower complication rate (no osteotomy hardware)
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1437; AO Foundation

3. Triceps-Splitting (Campbell) Approach

  • Midline split of triceps tendon and medial head of triceps
  • Can be extended distally by splitting triceps insertion on olecranon
  • Medial & lateral full-thickness fasciotaneous flaps raised
  • For TEA: olecranon tip resected, collateral ligaments released for prosthesis insertion
  • For ORIF: MCL and LCL preserved (asterisk structures)
  • Moderate articular visualization; acceptable for C1/C2
R&G 10th Ed., Fig. 39-21, p. 1438

4. Lateral (Kocher) Approach

  • Indication: Isolated lateral condyle fractures, capitellum fractures (B1, B3), lateral column fixation
  • Interval: between ECU and anconeus (Kocher interval)
  • Identify FCU, ECU, EDC, LCL, anconeus, triceps
  • Protect LUCL — do not detach from lateral epicondyle (risk of posterolateral instability)
  • If LUCL detached for exposure → mandatory repair at closure
  • PIN at risk distally — pronate forearm to protect
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1439; AO Foundation

Complications

Stiffness / Loss of Motion

  • Most common complication — up to 50% lose some arc
  • Functional arc: 30–130° flexion, 50° pro/supination
  • Causes: periarticular ossification, capsular contracture, inadequate fixation preventing early motion
  • Treatment: manipulation under anesthesia, arthroscopic/open capsular release
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1453

Ulnar Neuropathy

  • Incidence: 10–15% perioperative
  • Mostly neuropraxia — resolves in majority
  • Routine anterior transposition: no consensus; some advocate in situ decompression
  • If transposed, ensure no kinking at intermuscular septum
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1454

Heterotopic Ossification (HO)

  • Incidence: 5–49% radiographic; less clinical
  • Risk factors: head injury, burns, delayed fixation, extensive stripping
  • Prophylaxis: indomethacin or limited-field radiation (controversial)
  • Excision after maturation (12–18 months)
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1455

Non-union / Malunion

  • Non-union rate: 2–10% after ORIF
  • Causes: inadequate fixation, infection, osteoporosis
  • Treatment: revision ORIF + bone graft; TEA if poor bone stock
  • Olecranon osteotomy non-union: 2–5%
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1456

Infection

  • Superficial: 1–7%; deep: 1–3%
  • Risk: open fractures, prolonged surgery, immunocompromise
  • Treatment: debridement, antibiotics, retention if fixation stable
  • Late infection → removal + TEA or fusion
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1455

Implant Failure / Loosening

  • ORIF: screw backout, plate breakage if early mobilization without solid construct
  • TEA loosening: 5–10% at 10 years; activity restriction mandatory
  • TEA bushing wear: most common late complication
R&G 10th Ed., p. 1452
