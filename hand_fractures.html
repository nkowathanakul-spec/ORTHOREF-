Hand Fractures & Dislocations

Phalanges · Metacarpals · CMC Joint

Epidemiology

  • Phalangeal and metacarpal fractures are the second most common fractures after distal forearm fractures
  • Phalangeal fractures: 12.5 per 10,000 persons per year; metacarpal fractures: 8.4 per 10,000 per year
  • Peak incidence in the 2nd–4th decades of life
  • Males more likely to sustain metacarpal and phalangeal fractures; females more prone to distal forearm fractures
  • Higher incidence in patients with lower socioeconomic status

Key Principles

  • Hand fractures demand early mobilization — failure to mobilize by 4 weeks is detrimental to outcome
  • Malrotation is the most commonly missed deformity and should be assessed at every visit
  • All fingertips should point toward the scaphoid when the MCP joints are flexed
  • Splint in the intrinsic-plus (Edinburgh) position: wrist neutral–15° extension, MCP 70–90° flexion, IP joints in extension
  • This maintains MCP collateral ligament length and prevents volar plate contracture
Exam Pearl: Each 5° of malrotation at the proximal phalanx results in approximately 1.5 cm of digital overlap at the fingertip. Rotational alignment can only be judged with flexed MCP joints.

Imaging

  • Standard views: PA, lateral, oblique of the affected digit
  • Dedicated digit films are superior to hand views for detail
  • Brewerton view: dorsum of digits flat, MCP flexed 65°, beam 15° ulnar-to-radial — profiles the metacarpal recess and detects collateral ligament avulsions
  • CT with sagittal reconstructions valuable for complex articular base fractures (pilon)
  • Fluoroscopy useful for dynamic stability assessment

Distal Phalanx

  • Articulates proximally with the middle phalanx (digits) or proximal phalanx (thumb)
  • DIP/IP joint stabilized by proper and accessory collateral ligaments and the volar plate
  • FDP tendon inserts on the volar proximal fifth of the distal phalanx, ~1 mm distal to the articular surface
  • Terminal extensor tendon covers the dorsal proximal base
  • Nail matrix (germinal + sterile) overlies the dorsal surface; the nail plate provides additional structural support
  • The pulp has fibrous septations connecting the skin to the volar and distal phalanx — relevant to tuft fracture pain and subungual hematoma
  • Three anatomic regions: tuft (distal), shaft (middle), base (proximal attachment for ligaments and tendons)

Middle Phalanx — Deforming Forces

  • Deformity pattern depends on fracture location relative to the FDS insertion
  • Fractures proximal to FDS insertion → apex dorsal deformity (FDS flexes distal fragment, central slip extends proximal fragment)
  • Fractures distal to FDS insertion → apex volar deformity (FDS flexes proximal fragment, terminal extensor extends distal fragment)
Fig 45-10/11 (R&G): Top — Deforming forces on middle phalanx shaft fractures relative to the FDS insertion. Bottom — PIP joint stability: volar rim fragments >40% of the articular surface are at increasing risk of dorsal instability.
Exam Pearl — PIP Joint Stability: Volar lip fractures involving >40% of the articular surface place the PIP joint at risk for dorsal instability. Fractures involving >50% of the surface or requiring >30° of flexion to maintain reduction require operative treatment.

Proximal Phalanx — Deforming Forces

  • Base fractures → apex volar deformity: central slip extends the distal fragment, intrinsics flex the proximal fragment
  • Shaft fractures are inherently unstable owing to few stabilizing soft-tissue attachments
  • Neck fractures → apex volar angulation with dorsal displacement of the distal fragment
  • Condylar fractures involve shear mechanisms; collateral ligaments act as deforming forces

Metacarpal Anatomy

  • Five tubular bones forming the skeletal framework of the palm
  • 2nd and 3rd MC have rigid, relatively immobile CMC joints — less tolerance for angulation
  • 4th and 5th MC have 15–30° of CMC motion — compensate for greater angular deformity at the neck
  • Interossei originate from adjacent metacarpal shafts; disruption during plating risks intrinsic contracture
  • The deep transverse metacarpal ligament connects MC heads 2–5, providing stability and limiting independent metacarpal motion

Thumb CMC Joint Ligaments

  • The primary stabilizers of the thumb CMC joint include four ligaments:
  • Deep anterior oblique (beak) ligament — the most important stabilizer, resists dorsal subluxation
  • Dorsoradial ligament — resists dorsal translation
  • Posterior oblique ligament — secondary stabilizer
  • Intermetacarpal ligament — connects 1st to 2nd MC base
  • Superficial anterior oblique and ulnar collateral ligaments are not primary stabilizers

Distal Phalanx Fractures

Tuft Fracture (Fig 45-1C)

  • Distal-most segment; usually from crush injuries
  • Simple or comminuted patterns
  • Associated with subungual hematoma, nail bed laceration
  • Soft tissue integrity determines stability, not the bony pattern
  • Treatment: protective splint 4–6 weeks; decompress subungual hematoma if painful

Shaft Fracture (Fig 45-1A,B)

  • Transverse or longitudinal split patterns
  • Stable if soft tissues intact; unstable if periosteum/nail bed disrupted
  • Stable: splint DIP in extension 4 weeks
  • Unstable / displaced: CRPP with 0.028–0.035″ K-wires or 18-gauge hypodermic needle

Mallet Fracture — Dorsal Base (Fig 45-1D,E)

  • Avulsion of the terminal extensor tendon insertion with a dorsal bony fragment
  • Type I: no DIP joint subluxation → nonoperative (splint DIP in full extension × 6 weeks)
  • Type II: DIP joint subluxation → operative (extension block pinning)
  • Type III: epiphyseal injury (pediatric)
  • Crawford outcomes: excellent = full extension/flexion, no pain; good = <10° lag; fair = 10–25° lag; poor = >25° lag + pain

Jersey Finger — Volar Base (Fig 45-1F)

  • Avulsion of the FDP tendon from the volar base of the distal phalanx
  • Mechanism: forced extension against actively flexed DIP joint (grabbing a jersey)
  • Ring finger most commonly affected
  • Urgent surgical treatment required, ideally within 2 weeks
  • Repair via volar Bruner approach: pull-out wire/suture or bone anchor fixation

Complete Articular Base Fracture (Fig 45-1G)

  • Fracture splitting the entire articular surface of the distal phalanx base
  • Often associated with both FDP and extensor disruption
  • Requires operative reduction and fixation

Middle Phalanx Fractures

Condylar Fractures

  • Unicondylar (partial articular) or bicondylar (complete articular)
  • Low threshold for operative fixation due to articular involvement
  • Minimum 2 K-wires to prevent fragment rotation

Neck Fractures

  • Transverse extra-articular fractures proximal to the condyles
  • Usually present with angulation and instability requiring reduction and fixation
  • Rarely, distal fragment rotated 180° requiring open approach

Shaft Fractures

  • Deformity pattern depends on relationship to FDS insertion
  • Transverse, oblique, spiral, or comminuted
  • CRPP preferred over plates to minimize tendon adhesions

Volar Base Fracture — PIP Fracture-Dislocation

  • The critical threshold is >40% articular involvement → dorsal subluxation risk
  • Stable (<30% surface): extension block splinting
  • Tenuous (30–50%, stable in <30° flexion): extension block splint or pinning
  • Unstable (>50% or requires >30° flexion to maintain reduction): operative fixation
  • Options: extension block pinning, dynamic traction, force couple splint, volar plate arthroplasty, hemi-hamate arthroplasty

Dorsal Base Fracture (Bony Boutonnière)

  • Avulsion of the central slip insertion
  • If congruent joint: splint PIP in extension 4–6 weeks, allow DIP flexion
  • If displaced with joint incongruity: operative fixation (lag screw or K-wires)

Pilon Fractures

  • Complete articular fracture of the middle phalanx base
  • Often axially unstable with significant impaction
  • Nonoperative treatment yields suboptimal results → operative treatment recommended
  • Options: dynamic traction external fixation, ORIF with mini-plates
Proximal Phalanx Fractures

Proximal Phalanx Condylar Fractures

  • Shear mechanism; collateral ligaments are the primary deforming force
  • Articular congruity must be restored
  • Minimum 2 K-wires for rotational control

Proximal Phalanx Neck Fractures

  • Apex volar angulation with dorsal displacement of the distal fragment
  • Reduction by hyperflexion of the PIP joint
  • Retrograde transarticular K-wire, then cross pins from condylar recess

Proximal Phalanx Shaft Fractures

  • Typical deformity: apex volar (intrinsics flex proximal fragment, central slip extends distal fragment)
  • Inherently unstable due to few soft-tissue attachments
  • Surgical indications: angulation >25° sagittal, >15° coronal, malrotation, displacement
  • Fixation: antegrade or retrograde K-wires, lag screws for oblique fractures, plates for comminution

Proximal Phalanx Base Fractures

  • If nondisplaced/reducible → splinting or buddy taping
  • If apex volar angulation >20–30° → surgery to prevent pseudoclawing
  • Options: CRPP (cross pins from base) or ORIF
Metacarpal Fractures

Metacarpal Head Fractures

  • Intra-articular; mechanism usually direct impact or fight-bite
  • Best seen on Brewerton view
  • Nondisplaced: immobilization with MCP in 70° flexion
  • Displaced: ORIF with headless compression screws or K-wires
  • Fight-bite wounds: irrigate, debride, antibiotics — do not close primarily

Metacarpal Neck Fracture — "Boxer's Fracture"

  • Most common metacarpal fracture; 5th MC most frequently affected
  • Mechanism: axial load with MCP flexed (punching)
  • Apex dorsal angulation; reduction with the Jahss maneuver
  • Acceptable sagittal angulation thresholds:
  • • 2nd MC: 10–15°
  • • 3rd MC: 10–15°
  • • 4th MC: 20–30°
  • • 5th MC: 40–50° (some authors accept up to 70°)
  • Greater tolerance in ring/small fingers due to 15–30° CMC compensatory motion
  • No malrotation is acceptable in any metacarpal

Metacarpal Shaft Fractures

  • Transverse, oblique, spiral, or comminuted patterns
  • Deformity: apex dorsal angulation (interossei flex, extensors bow-string)
  • Acceptable angulation: 10° for 2nd/3rd, 20° for 4th/5th
  • No shortening >3–4 mm; no malrotation
  • Operative options: K-wires (transverse/bouquet), lag screws (oblique/spiral), plate and screws (comminuted), intramedullary devices

Metacarpal Base Fractures (2nd–5th)

  • Often stable due to strong dorsal and interosseous ligaments
  • 4th and 5th MC base fractures may have associated CMC dislocation (reverse Bennett pattern)
  • CT useful for articular involvement assessment
  • Nondisplaced: ulnar gutter splint; displaced: CRPP or ORIF

Bennett Fracture (Fig 45-69A)

  • Partial articular fracture of the thumb metacarpal base
  • Two-part fracture: small volar-ulnar fragment remains attached to trapezium via the deep anterior oblique (beak) ligament
  • The metacarpal shaft displaces dorsally, radially, and proximally due to:
  • • APL: proximal migration
  • • Adductor pollicis: adduction and supination
  • Operative treatment required: CRPP (percutaneous pinning to trapezium/2nd MC) or ORIF
  • Articular step-off >1–2 mm generally requires anatomic reduction

Rolando Fracture (Fig 45-69B)

  • Complete articular fracture of the thumb MC base — classically a Y- or T-shaped pattern
  • More comminuted than Bennett; includes both volar and dorsal fragments
  • If fragments are large enough: ORIF with mini-screws/plate
  • If highly comminuted: external fixation ± limited K-wire fixation, or primary arthrodesis in salvage

MCP Joint Dislocations

Simple (Incomplete) MCP Dislocation

  • Subluxation that is easily reduced by closed means
  • Finger appears more hyperextended than in a complete dislocation (proximal phalanx still contacts MC head)
  • Reduce with longitudinal traction and volar translation

Complex (Complete) MCP Dislocation

  • Irreducible due to interposed structures ("noose" sign for index finger)
  • Index finger noose: flexor tendon medially, lumbrical laterally, natatory ligament distally, superficial transverse MC ligament proximally
  • Look for skin dimpling palmarly — pathognomonic
  • Dorsal sesamoid on x-ray is a classic sign
  • Requires open reduction (volar or dorsal approach)
  • Do NOT hyperextend further as this tightens the noose

Thumb UCL Injury — Skier's/Gamekeeper's Thumb

  • Acute (skier's) vs chronic (gamekeeper's) UCL injury
  • Mechanism: forced abduction/radial deviation at thumb MCP
  • Grade I: pain, no laxity; Grade II: partial tear, firm endpoint; Grade III: complete rupture, no endpoint
  • Stener lesion: displaced UCL lies superficial to the adductor aponeurosis — cannot heal without surgery
  • Stress test: >30° of radial deviation or >15° more than contralateral → complete tear
  • Grade III / Stener lesion → operative repair or reconstruction

Distal Phalanx Fractures

Nonoperative Treatment

  • Tuft/shaft: volar DIP splint in extension × 4–6 weeks
  • Subungual hematoma: drain with heated 18-gauge needle or electrocautery
  • Open tuft fractures: irrigate and debride in clinic under digital block; repair nail bed with 5-0 or 6-0 absorbable suture on a noncutting tapered needle; replace nail plate under the fold
  • Mallet fracture without DIP subluxation: splint DIP in full extension × 6 weeks; patient must not flex DIP during this period

Operative Treatment

  • Displaced shaft: CRPP with 0.028–0.035″ K-wires
  • Mallet with DIP subluxation (Type II): extension block pinning — transarticular K-wire in 5° extension + dorsal blocking pin in middle phalanx head
  • FDP avulsion (Jersey finger): volar Bruner approach → pull-out wire through dorsal button, or bone anchor/screw fixation; ideally repaired within 2 weeks
  • Postop: volar splint immobilizing DIP × 4 weeks, then pin removal
Mallet splinting tip: Multiple splint designs work equally well. The key is continuous DIP extension — even a single episode of uncontrolled flexion resets the healing clock. Weekly radiographic follow-up is recommended.

Middle Phalanx Fractures

Nonoperative

  • Nondisplaced neck and shaft fractures: dorsal/volar gutter splint immobilizing PIP + DIP in extension × 4 weeks
  • Active ROM with buddy taping at 4 weeks, discontinued at 6 weeks
  • Volar base fractures (<30% articular surface): extension block splinting — block extension at 30°, increase 10° per week, discontinue at 4 weeks
  • Dorsal base fractures (congruent joint): splint PIP in extension 4–6 weeks, allow DIP flexion

Operative

  • Condylar fractures: CRPP with minimum 2 K-wires perpendicular to fracture
  • Shaft fractures: CRPP preferred; transverse → cross K-wires; oblique/spiral → perpendicular K-wires or lag screws
  • Plate fixation (1.5 mm screws, 2 screws each side) reserved for comminution; associated with higher adhesion risk
  • Volar base (>50% surface or >30° flexion to maintain): extension block pinning, dynamic traction, volar plate arthroplasty, or hemi-hamate arthroplasty

Proximal Phalanx Fractures

Nonoperative

  • Intrinsic-plus splint: wrist 30° extension, MCP ≥70° flexion, IP joints in extension
  • Continuous splint 3–4 weeks → removable splint with buddy taping and mobilization
  • Criteria: no malrotation, angulation <25° sagittal and <10° coronal

Operative Indications

  • Malrotation/scissoring
  • Sagittal angulation >25°, coronal angulation >15°
  • Base fractures with apex volar angulation >20–30° (risk of pseudoclawing)
  • Displaced condylar fractures, intra-articular step-off
  • Open fractures, concomitant tendon/nerve injuries

Metacarpal Fractures

Nonoperative

  • Neck fractures within acceptable angulation (see Classification tab): Jahss maneuver reduction → ulnar gutter splint (MCP 70–90° flexion, IP free) × 3–4 weeks
  • Shaft fractures: acceptable if shortening <3–4 mm, no malrotation, angulation within limits
  • Buddy taping and early motion after initial splinting period

Operative

  • Neck fractures beyond limits: CRPP or intramedullary K-wire fixation (see Approaches tab)
  • Shaft fractures: transverse → cross K-wires or intramedullary nails; oblique/spiral → lag screws (minimum 2); comminuted → plate and screws
  • Bennett fracture: closed reduction + percutaneous pinning (K-wire to trapezium or 2nd MC) if articular congruity maintained; ORIF if step-off >1–2 mm or unstable
  • Rolando fracture: ORIF if fragments amenable; external fixation ± K-wires if comminuted
Fight-Bite Warning: Any laceration over the MCP joint after a punch must be treated as a human bite until proven otherwise. Irrigate, debride, leave open, culture, and start antibiotics covering oral flora (Eikenella corrodens). Delayed presentation → higher risk for septic arthritis and osteomyelitis.

MCP Dislocations

  • Simple: gentle longitudinal traction + volar translation; do NOT hyperextend
  • Complex (irreducible): open reduction — dorsal approach preferred (avoids neurovascular injury)
  • Thumb UCL Grade III / Stener lesion: operative repair with bone anchor or transosseous suture
  • Post-reduction: buddy taping, early motion if stable; transarticular K-wire if unstable × 3–4 weeks

Surgical Approaches to the Phalanges

Midaxial Approach to the Middle Phalanx

  • Incision along the dorsal edge of the flexion creases connecting PIP and DIP
  • Neurovascular bundle retracted volarly
  • Identify the lateral band proximally → retract dorsally for access to the distal half of the phalanx
  • If more proximal access needed, incise the lateral band in line with the skin incision → access to the middle phalanx base and PIP joint
Fig 45-19 (R&G): Midaxial approach to the middle phalanx. A: Incision markings. B: NV bundle retracted volarly. C: Lateral band retracted dorsally. D: Lateral band incised for proximal access.

Dorsal Approach to the Middle Phalanx

  • Longitudinal incision in line with the axis of the digit, dorsal midline
  • Elevate skin flaps; preserve longitudinal veins
  • Identify the extensor mechanism
  • Incise the triangular ligament to gain access to the distal phalanx

Intramedullary K-Wire Fixation — Metacarpal Neck Fractures

AO Foundation technique for extraarticular metacarpal neck fractures using antegrade intramedullary K-wires inserted through the metacarpal base.

Step 1 / 7
Aftercare (AO): If swelling present, apply a dorsal splint in intrinsic-plus position (wrist neutral–15° extension, MCP 90° flexion, PIP extension) for 1 week. Begin active flexion/extension immediately. Buddy strap after swelling subsides. Suture removal at 10–12 days with x-ray check. K-wire removal at ~6 weeks after confirming fracture consolidation.

Stiffness

  • The most common complication of all hand fractures
  • Caused by prolonged immobilization, tendon adhesions, collateral ligament contracture, and volar plate contracture
  • Risk increased when MCP joint is immobilized in extension (collateral ligaments shorten)
  • Prevention: immobilize in intrinsic-plus position, minimize immobilization duration, early protected motion
  • Failure to mobilize by 4 weeks is detrimental to final outcome
  • Concomitant extensor tendon injuries portend worse outcomes than flexor tendon injuries for stiffness

Malrotation / Malunion

  • Malrotation is the most commonly missed deformity — often only apparent with MCP joints flexed
  • Proximal phalanx malunion classified into four subtypes: malrotation, apex volar angulation, lateral angulation, shortening
  • Malrotation correction: transverse derotational osteotomy at the fracture site or metacarpal base; place 2 parallel K-wires for rotational reference before osteotomy
  • Apex volar angulation >20–30° → pseudoclawing → opening or closing wedge osteotomy
  • Shortening rarely requires osteotomy; more commonly produces a prominent volar bone spike limiting PIP flexion → excise from volar approach

Tendon Adhesions

  • Higher risk with plate fixation (dorsal or lateral) compared to K-wire fixation
  • Extensor tendon adhesions more common and more symptomatic than flexor
  • Minimized by anatomic reduction, low-profile implants, careful soft-tissue handling, and early motion
  • Tenolysis considered if plateau in motion after 3–6 months of therapy

Infection

  • Pin-tract infection: daily cleaning with antiseptic; most resolve with oral antibiotics and pin removal
  • Fight-bite infections: Eikenella corrodens is pathognomonic; polymicrobial common; requires I&D and IV antibiotics
  • Open distal phalanx fractures: evidence suggests prophylactic antibiotics are not necessary (low infection risk given good vascularity)

Nonunion

  • Uncommon in hand fractures due to excellent vascularity
  • Distal phalanx shaft nonunions occasionally seen; most are asymptomatic
  • Symptomatic nonunions: bone grafting + compression screw fixation
  • Predictive factor for nonunion in distal phalanx: fracture gapping (not K-wire construct)

Swan-Neck Deformity

  • Complication of volar base fractures / mallet injuries at the PIP and DIP joints
  • Loss of DIP extension → compensatory PIP hyperextension via central slip imbalance
  • Reported in one-third of patients without subluxation and two-thirds of patients with subluxation after mallet injuries

Post-Traumatic Arthritis

  • Risk proportional to articular incongruity and residual subluxation
  • Reported in 18% of hemi-hamate arthroplasty cases
  • Treatment: NSAIDs, therapy, corticosteroid injections, arthrodesis for refractory cases

Metacarpal-Specific Complications

  • Extensor lag: from residual dorsal angulation at the MC neck — cosmetic "loss of knuckle" prominence
  • Grip strength reduction: MC shortening alters intrinsic muscle tension and grip mechanics
  • Dorsal prominence: malreduced Bennett/Rolando fractures → painful CMC arthritis over time
  • K-wire migration/backing out: pre-bend wires at entry portal and cut flush or bury beneath skin
