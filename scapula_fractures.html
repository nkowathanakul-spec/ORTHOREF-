Epidemiology & Mechanism

Scapula fractures are uncommon injuries, representing fewer than 1% of all fractures and approximately 3–5% of shoulder girdle injuries. The deep muscular investment of the scapula provides significant protection, meaning high-energy trauma is typically required.

The scapular body is the most frequently fractured region (~50%), followed by the glenoid neck (~25%), glenoid fossa (~10%), and processes (~15%). Bilateral scapula fractures should prompt concern for non-accidental injury in the pediatric population.

Pearl The scapula is surrounded by 17 muscles on all sides. High-energy trauma is required to fracture it — always search aggressively for associated injuries.
Mechanism

High-Energy Trauma

  • Direct blow to the posterior shoulder (most common)
  • Motor vehicle accident — front-seat occupant
  • Fall from height
  • Axial loading through the arm (glenoid fractures)
Associated Injuries

~90% Have Concomitant Injuries

  • Rib fractures — most common (50–98%)
  • Pneumothorax / hemothorax
  • Brachial plexus injury (5–13%)
  • Clavicle fracture → "floating shoulder"
  • Subclavian / axillary vessel injury
  • Head / spine injury
Hazard The scapula's deep muscular protection means a scapula fracture signals massive energy transfer. Life-threatening thoracic injuries must be excluded before operative planning.
02 —

Surgical Anatomy

The scapula is a complex, triangular flat bone with thin cortices traversed by trabecular struts. Three bony pillars resist deformation: the lateral pillar (axillary border), the medial pillar, and the spinal pillar.

The glenopolar angle (GPA) is measured on AP radiograph as the angle between the line connecting superior-inferior poles of the glenoid and the line connecting the superior glenoid pole to the inferior scapular angle. Normal GPA = 30–45°; a GPA <20° indicates significant neck angulation requiring surgical consideration.

Key Landmark — Coracoid The coracoid process is the most important landmark for the deltopectoral approach. The musculocutaneous nerve enters coracobrachialis as close as 2.5 cm distal to the tip — excessive medial retraction must be avoided.

Clinical Evaluation

History

  • Mechanism — high-energy vs. low-energy (pathological fracture?)
  • Hand dominance and functional demands
  • Pre-existing shoulder pathology
  • Neurological symptoms — numbness, weakness (brachial plexus)
  • Respiratory symptoms — pneumothorax, rib fractures

Physical Examination

  • Inspection: bruising, deformity, asymmetry
  • Tenderness: localize to body, neck, glenoid, processes
  • ROM: typically very limited due to pain
  • Neurovascular: axillary nerve (lateral arm sensation + deltoid), musculocutaneous nerve, brachial plexus
  • Chest: auscultation for pneumothorax, rib tenderness
Hazard — Missed Injuries Do NOT focus only on the shoulder. A systematic ATLS primary and secondary survey is mandatory. Up to 90% of patients have associated injuries — rib fractures, pneumothorax, and brachial plexus injuries are most common.
Neurological Examination Test axillary nerve (lateral arm sensation, deltoid contraction) and musculocutaneous nerve (biceps, lateral forearm sensation). Document before any intervention. Suprascapular nerve injury can be missed acutely.
Floating Shoulder Ipsilateral clavicle fracture + scapula neck fracture = "floating shoulder." The superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC) is disrupted at two points. Surgical stabilization of at least one element (usually the clavicle) is generally recommended.
05 —

Imaging & Radiologic Assessment

Step 01
Standard Shoulder Series
True AP (Grashey), scapular Y, axillary lateral. The axillary view is critical to assess glenohumeral congruity and glenoid fracture displacement. Obtain as part of the initial trauma evaluation.
Step 02
Chest Radiograph
Mandatory to evaluate pneumothorax, hemothorax, rib fractures, mediastinal injury, and contralateral shoulder. The scapula is often incidentally identified on the chest X-ray.
Step 03
CT Scan with 3D Reconstruction
Essential for all scapula fractures. CT defines: fracture pattern and comminution, glenoid articular step-off, GPA measurement, neck angulation, medial translation, and associated chest injuries. 3D reconstructions are invaluable for surgical planning.
Step 04
Glenopolar Angle (GPA) Measurement
Measured on AP/Grashey view. Normal = 30–45°. A GPA <20° indicates severe neck angulation and is a relative indication for surgery. Associated with medialization of the glenoid and functional impairment of the rotator cuff.
Step 05
MRI / Arteriography (Selective)
MRI if brachial plexus or rotator cuff injury suspected. CTA/arteriography if vascular injury (expanding hematoma, absent pulses, ABI <0.9) is suspected — axillary artery injury is rare but life-threatening.
06 —

Non-Operative Treatment

The majority (~85%) of scapula fractures are amenable to non-operative management with excellent long-term outcomes, given the stabilizing effect of surrounding musculature.

Indications for Non-Operative Treatment

  • Scapular body fractures (most cases)
  • Minimally displaced glenoid neck fractures (GPA >20°, translation <10 mm)
  • Acromion fractures without rotator cuff impingement
  • Coracoid fractures (most are undisplaced)
  • Medically unfit for surgery
  • Low-demand patients

Non-Operative Protocol

  • Sling: 2–4 weeks for comfort, not strict immobilization
  • Early pendulum exercises as pain allows (72 hrs)
  • Progressive ROM at 3–4 weeks: forward elevation, ER
  • Strengthening at 6–8 weeks once pain resolves
  • Return to full activity by 3–4 months
  • Repeat radiographs at 2, 6, and 12 weeks
Outcome Data Scapular body fractures treated non-operatively have reported good-to-excellent outcomes in 80–95% of patients. Persistent shoulder dysfunction correlates most with brachial plexus injury, not the fracture itself.
07 —

Operative Indications

GPA Threshold GPA <20° — significant medialization and inferior tilt of glenoid; predictive of rotator cuff dysfunction and poor functional outcome
Medial Translation Glenoid medial displacement >20–25 mm from the intact side
Glenoid Step-Off Intra-articular displacement >4 mm with glenohumeral incongruity (Ideberg Types II–V)
Floating Shoulder Ipsilateral clavicle + scapula neck fracture with displacement — ORIF of clavicle (at minimum) to restore SSSC integrity
Acromion / Spine Displaced acromion fractures with subacromial impingement on rotator cuff; comminuted spine fractures after reverse arthroplasty
Open Fractures Open injuries require emergent irrigation and débridement ± fixation
Vascular Injury Axillary artery injury — emergent surgical repair with fracture stabilization

Glenoid Neck Fixation (Posterior Approach)

  • Patient in lateral decubitus or prone
  • Judet posterior approach — internervous plane between teres minor and infraspinatus
  • Plate fixation along the lateral pillar (3.5 mm reconstruction or locking plate)
  • Additional screws through the scapular spine column if needed

Anterior Glenoid ORIF (Deltopectoral)

  • Anterior glenoid rim fractures (Ideberg I) causing instability
  • Deltopectoral approach with subscapularis tenotomy
  • Buttress plating or lag screw fixation of glenoid rim
  • Repair subscapularis at lesser tuberosity with transosseous sutures

Complications

Shoulder Stiffness

Most common complication (~25%). Early mobilization reduces risk. Adhesive capsulitis may develop after prolonged immobilization; treat with physiotherapy ± manipulation under anaesthesia.

Malunion / Angular Deformity

GPA <20° at union correlates with rotator cuff dysfunction and reduced abduction strength. Late corrective osteotomy is rarely required but technically demanding.

Brachial Plexus Injury

Present in 5–13% at injury (not iatrogenic). Prognosis depends on injury severity (neurapraxia vs. rupture vs. avulsion). EMG/NCS at 3–6 weeks to guide further management.

Axillary Nerve Palsy

Iatrogenic during approach. Presents as deltoid weakness and lateral arm numbness. Risk minimized by avoiding inferior retractors below the 6 o'clock glenoid position.

Musculocutaneous Nerve Injury

Neurapraxia from vigorous medial retraction of conjoined tendon. Most resolve within 3–6 months. Permanent damage possible if nerve is sharply divided.

Post-Traumatic Arthritis

Follows intra-articular glenoid fractures with residual step-off >4 mm. Risk reduced by anatomic reduction and stable fixation. Total shoulder arthroplasty if severe.

Subscapularis Failure

After deltopectoral approach; leads to anterior instability and weakness of internal rotation. Requires primary repair — do not leave untreated.

Nonunion

Rare for body fractures given rich vascular supply. More common with process fractures (acromion, coracoid). Symptomatic nonunion requires ORIF with bone grafting.

Suprascapular Nerve Entrapment

Can be entrapped at the suprascapular notch by callus or hematoma. Presents as posterior shoulder pain and supraspinatus / infraspinatus atrophy weeks to months later.
