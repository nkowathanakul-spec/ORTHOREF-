02Pathoanatomy & Applied Anatomy

The femur is a tubular long bone with posterior cortical thickening — the linea aspera — the site of fascial and muscular attachment and entry of the nutrient vessel. It has an anterolateral bow (radius of curvature ≈ 120 cm). Metaphyseal flares proximally (subtrochanteric) and distally are stress-concentration zones.

Deforming forces & muscle attachments

Proximal third

Proximal fragment flexes & externally rotates (iliopsoas + short external rotators) and abducts (gluteals).

Distal / meta-diaphyseal

Shaft medializes (adductor pull); gastrocnemius on the posterior condyles produces an extension deformity of the distal fragment.

Shortening occurs at almost any location from hamstring and quadriceps contraction. Understanding these vectors lets the surgeon plan reduction rather than relying on straight in-line traction alone.

Compartments of the thigh

Three compartments separated by medial, lateral, and posterior septa:

  • Anterior — quadriceps, sartorius, iliacus, psoas, pectineus; femoral artery/vein, femoral nerve, lateral femoral cutaneous nerve
  • Medial — gracilis, adductors longus/brevis/magnus, obturator externus; profunda femoris, obturator artery/vein/nerve
  • Posterior — biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus, part of adductor magnus; profunda perforators, sciatic nerve, posterior femoral cutaneous nerve

Blood supply

  • External iliac → femoral artery (anterior compartment) → profunda femoris gives medial/lateral circumflex (head & neck) and perforators along the whole shaft
  • Perforators course ~every 3 cm and must be cauterized/ligated in the lateral approach
  • Femoral artery passes the adductor hiatus → popliteal artery
  • Primary nutrient artery (from profunda) enters at the linea aspera and supplies the inner ⅓ cortex — limit stripping here
  • After medullary disruption (reaming/nail/displaced fracture), periosteal flow reverses to revascularize the inner cortex

03Classification

No universally accepted system exists. Fractures are described by location (proximal / middle / distal third; the isthmus lies in the middle third, so distal fractures are "infraisthmal"), pattern (transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted, segmental), and comminution. Anatomy of each third affects how well an IMN fills the canal and controls alignment.

04Management

Gold standardReamed, statically locked intramedullary nailing. Very few femoral shaft fractures cannot be nailed. Debate = starting point, reaming, nail type.

Nonoperative

  • Indications: children <5 yr (spica) · too sick for surgery · prohibitive comorbidity · resource-limited setting
  • Skeletal traction: distal femoral pin medial→lateral (protect femoral artery) or proximal tibial pin lateral→medial (protect peroneal nerve); ~15–20 lb / 15% body weight
  • Cast bracing: largely historical — knee stiffness is the main problem after prolonged traction

Intramedullary nailing

Antegrade — piriformis entry

  • Too anterior → hoop stress / neck fx
  • Too lateral → varus
  • Injures piriformis/obturator tendons; risks MFCA & superior gluteal nerve

  • Mismatch → iatrogenic fx / varus

Reamed vs unreamed

  • Reaming: endosteal flow ↓~83% but periosteal flow ↑ 6× and ~24% reamings deposited at fracture
  • Reaming favored — lower nonunion (COTS 1.7% vs 7.5%), less implant failure, faster union; no ↑ ARDS/mortality
  • Screw holes >50% nail diameter markedly weaken it (5–6 mm screws typical)

05Surgical Approaches

The lateral (vastus lateralis) approach is the workhorse extensile exposure of the femoral shaft — for open plating, direct reduction, and access to the diaphysis. It can be extended along nearly the entire femur. Walk through it below.

Pearl
Hazard

Approach principles

  • Develop the plane anterior to the lateral intermuscular septum; elevate vastus lateralis off the septum from posterior
  • Ligate/cauterize perforators (~every 3 cm) as they cross to the septum — retracted vessels bleed occultly
  • Limit stripping at the linea aspera to protect the nutrient artery
  • Paralyze the patient for reduction; use distal femoral traction pin, femoral distractor, or Schanz-pin joysticks to restore length
  • Fluoroscopy from the side opposite the surgeon; confirm length, alignment, rotation and re-check the femoral neck

06Complications

Nerve injury

Most are iatrogenic from positioning — pudendal (perineal post traction), peroneal (opposite-limb leg holder), sciatic/femoral. Careful positioning, avoid excessive traction; treat expectantly with observation. Document a detailed pre-op exam.

Muscle weakness

From injury and surgery. Antegrade nailing injures hip abductors/external rotators; the quadriceps is most commonly weakened long-term. Vastus lateralis is partially dissected in the lateral plating approach.

Angular malalignment

Defined as > 5° coronal or sagittal. Uncommon after IMN because the isthmal fit controls alignment — but a problem in proximal/distal fractures where the nail does not fill the canal (use blocking screws / correct starting point).

Rotational deformity

The most common IMN malalignment: >10° in up to 40%, >15° in up to 19%. Up to ~15° is generally well tolerated; >15° with symptoms → revise the locking bolts. Use the cortical step sign, lesser-trochanter profile, and contralateral anteversion match.

Knee stiffness, knee & hip pain

Some knee stiffness is expected and usually temporary; correlates with injury energy, head injury/HO, and approach. Antegrade nailing → hip/trochanteric pain in 10%–40%.

Heterotopic ossification

Incidence 9%–60%, rarely symptomatic. Strongest risk factor is head injury; also male sex, prolonged intubation, surgical delay. Reamed antegrade nailing > unreamed for HO at the entry site (36% vs 9%).

Compartment syndrome

Rare but limb-threatening (~1%–2%)Maintain a high index of suspicion. Pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch establish the diagnosis — do not wait for pulselessness/pallor/paresthesia.

Refracture

Rare after fixation. Highest risk at the ends of the construct (end of nail/plate stress riser). Persistent end-of-plate pain on weight-bearing → consider implant removal.

Delayed union / nonunion

Low after reamed statically locked nailing — ~0.9%–1.1% in large series (higher in some reports, up to 6%–12.5%). Always consider occult infection as a cause. Options: dynamization, exchange reamed nailing (± blocking screws), augmentative plating, bone graft.

Infection & infected nonunion

Closed fractures < 1%; open fractures 2.4%–4.8%. Presentation ranges from florid (systemic symptoms, erythema, drainage) to indolent (nonunion only). May need debridement, implant management (± ex-fix "metal holiday"), and staged reconstruction.
