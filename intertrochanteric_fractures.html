Intertrochanteric Fractures

Trochanteric Fractures — Pertrochanteric Region
Rockwood & Green 10th Ed. · Ch. 54 AO Foundation · surgeryreference.aofoundation.org

Overview

~250K Annual US hip fractures; ~50% are intertrochanteric
50–25mm TAD target < 25 mm to prevent cut-out (Baumgaertner)
5cm Subtrochanteric zone: < 5 cm below LT (R&G Fig 54-1)
24–48h Surgical timing target — reduces mortality
R&G Fig 54-1 · Basic classification of proximal femur fractures. Intertrochanteric = extracapsular, between greater and lesser trochanters. Subtrochanteric extends ≤5 cm distal to lesser trochanter.

Definition & Epidemiology

  • Extracapsular fractures occurring between the greater and lesser trochanters along the intertrochanteric line
  • Blood supply to femoral head not at risk (unlike femoral neck fractures) — AVN is not a concern
  • Predominantly in elderly patients with low-energy falls; peak age 75–80 years
  • Osteoporosis is the primary risk factor; Singh index grades trabecular rarefaction
  • 30-day mortality: approximately 5–10%; 1-year mortality: 15–25%
  • High-energy mechanism in young patients: MVA, fall from height — associated injuries must be excluded

Key Clinical Points

Presentation

  • Shortened, externally rotated leg
  • Pain in groin/proximal thigh
  • Unable to bear weight
  • Ecchymosis develops over 24–48h

Imaging

  • AP pelvis + AP/lateral ipsilateral hip
  • Traction/internal rotation view delineates pattern
  • CT if comminution unclear or lateral wall concern
  • Contralateral hip for template/neck-shaft angle

⚠ Lateral Wall Integrity — Critical Decision Point

  • Lateral wall fracture = instability even in apparent A1 patterns
  • Lateral wall thickness < 20.5 mm predicts failure of SHS (Palm et al.)
  • Lateral wall involvement mandates cephalomedullary nail over SHS
  • Assess on AP radiograph from tip of greater trochanter distally

Surgical Anatomy

R&G Fig 54-5 · Trabecular architecture of the proximal femur. Ward's triangle (stress riser) and calcar femorale (Adam's arch) are key determinants of fracture stability and implant purchase.

Trabecular Architecture

Primary Compressive Trabeculae

  • Arise from medial cortex → superior femoral head
  • Thickest, strongest group
  • Best bone quality for implant purchase
  • Target for lag screw/helical blade positioning

Calcar Femorale (Adam's Arch)

  • Dense posteromedial cortical condensation
  • Runs from posteroinferior femoral neck to posteromedial femoral shaft
  • Most critical structure for intertrochanteric fracture stability
  • Disruption = unstable pattern (A2/A3)

Ward's Triangle

  • Region of low trabecular density between primary groups
  • Represents zone of stress concentration and fracture propagation
  • Tensile trabeculae pass between Ward's triangle and greater trochanter
  • Best bone quality: directly beneath articular surface of femoral head
R&G Fig 54-7 · Hip capsule anatomy. A: Anterior — Y-shaped iliofemoral ligament (ligament of Bigelow) and pubofemoral ligament. B: Posterior — ischiofemoral ligament, trochanteric crest. Intertrochanteric fractures are extracapsular — preserved capsular blood supply to femoral head.

Hip Capsule & Vascular Anatomy

Extracapsular Position

  • Hip capsule inserts at intertrochanteric line anteriorly
  • Posteriorly: capsule inserts proximal to intertrochanteric crest
  • Intertrochanteric fractures lie outside the capsule
  • Blood supply to femoral head via retinacular vessels is preserved
  • AVN is not a complication of this fracture pattern

Muscular Deforming Forces

  • Iliopsoas — flexion/ER of lesser trochanter fragment
  • Abductors (glut med/min) — proximal fragment abduction
  • Adductors — shaft medialization in A3
  • Short ER muscles pull trochanteric fragment posteriorly

Evans Classification (Historical)

  • Type I: undisplaced — stable
  • Type II: single fracture line, reducible — stable
  • Type III: single fracture line, irreducible — unstable
  • Type IV: comminuted — unstable
  • Type V: reverse oblique — unstable
  • AO/OTA system is now preferred for reproducibility and treatment guidance

Surgical Management

Operative Indications

  • Virtually all displaced intertrochanteric fractures require surgery
  • Nonoperative treatment reserved for truly non-ambulatory patients or prohibitive surgical risk
  • Early surgery (within 24–48h) reduces mortality, complications, and pain
  • Medical optimization should not delay surgery beyond 48h in most cases

Implant Selection

Reduction Principles

R&G Fig 54-25 · Correction of reduction on lateral view. Femoral head, neck, trochanteric region, and shaft should all be collinear. This alignment ensures optimal lag screw trajectory.

Acceptable Reduction

  • Neutral or slight valgus on AP view
  • Anterior cortex aligned on lateral view
  • No more than 20° of malrotation
  • Avoid varus — increases mechanical failure rate significantly

Reduction Maneuvers

  • Longitudinal traction on fracture table
  • Internal rotation 10–15° to correct ER deformity
  • Open reduction if closed fails: joystick technique
  • For A3: medial translation of shaft to restore alignment

Tip-Apex Distance (TAD)

R&G Fig 54-29 · Tip-apex distance (TAD) calculation. TAD = (X_AP × D_TRUE/D_AP) + (X_LAT × D_TRUE/D_LAT). The gold standard predictor of cut-out risk.
  • TAD < 25 mm: cut-out rate < 2% (Baumgaertner et al., 1995; confirmed R&G Ch. 54)
  • TAD > 45 mm: cut-out rate approaches 60%
  • Magnification correction mandatory: use known screw diameter as reference
  • Measure on both AP and lateral views and sum corrected distances

Cleveland Index / Femoral Head Position

Cleveland zones and calcar ratio for femoral head screw positioning. Center-center or center-inferior placement minimizes cut-out risk. Ratio AB/AC × 100 quantifies inferior displacement on lateral view.
  • Cleveland zones: divide femoral head into 9 zones (3×3 grid)
  • Optimal zones: Zone 5 (central AP + central lateral) or Zone 8 (central AP + inferior lateral)
  • Superior placement in any view is the highest risk position for cut-out
  • Inferior placement on lateral offloads rotational forces on the implant

Surgical Approach — Cephalomedullary Nailing

AO Foundation: Trochanteric fracture — Nailing (Short Nail). surgeryreference.aofoundation.org

Step Walkthrough

Lateral Decubitus Alternative

  • Used when fracture table unavailable or for revision situations
  • Requires manual traction assistant
  • Standard lateral approach to greater trochanter
  • Reduction more challenging but table positioning errors eliminated

Complications

Cut-Out (Most Common)

  • Lag screw penetrates superior femoral head
  • Risk factors: TAD > 25mm, varus reduction, superior placement
  • Treatment: revision with THA in elderly; re-fixation rarely possible
  • Prevention: TAD < 25mm strictly

Varus Malreduction

  • Most common intraoperative error
  • Increases implant bending moment
  • May lead to secondary loss of fixation
  • Accept neutral or slight valgus only

Lateral Wall Fracture

  • Occurs intraoperatively with SHS in thin lateral wall
  • Destabilizes the construct
  • Convert to cephalomedullary nail
  • Lateral wall < 20.5 mm: nail primarily

Implant Failure / Breakage

  • Nail or screw fatigue fracture
  • Usually from delayed union + cyclic loading
  • Risk: A3 patterns, osteoporosis, early WB
  • Revision: long nail + bone grafting

Nonunion

  • Uncommon (<2%) in standard patterns
  • Higher in A3, pathologic fractures
  • Treatment: revision fixation ± bone graft
  • THA if severe head involvement

Peri-Implant / Periprosthetic Fracture

  • Short nail creates stress riser at tip
  • Use long nail if isthmus or subtrochanteric concern
  • Overlap existing implant by ≥ 2 cortical diameters
  • Consider long nail in all high-energy young patients

Medical Complications

Early (<30 days)

  • DVT/PE — prophylaxis essential
  • Pressure ulcers (heel, sacrum)
  • Urinary tract infection — minimize catheter time
  • Delirium — common in elderly; multimodal prevention
  • Pneumonia — early mobilization key

Functional Recovery

  • Full weight-bearing as tolerated immediately post-op
  • Only ~50% of patients return to pre-injury function
  • Mortality 15–25% at 1 year
  • Geriatric co-management (orthogeriatrics) improves outcomes
  • Bone protection therapy: bisphosphonates post-operatively
