Ankle Fractures

Malleolar injuries — pathoanatomy, classification, surgical approaches and fixation strategy

Sources: Rockwood & Green's Fractures in Adults, 10th ed., Ch. 65 (White & Carter) · AO Foundation Surgery Reference (Malleoli)

A satisfactory outcome after ankle fracture can be anticipated when the joint is congruent — the talus sits anatomically under the plafond — and stable — it stays there until the fracture heals. Everything else is detail.

Epidemiology

Incidence, demographics & trends
  • Ankle fractures are the second most common lower limb fracture after hip fractures.
  • Bimodal distribution with peaks in younger men and older women, separated by a 50-year gap.
  • Typically low-energy — the majority from simple falls or sport. Even open ankle fractures are predominantly low-energy falls, with the highest incidence in elderly women.
  • Weber/OTA-AO type C fractures more often follow a fall from height or an RTA; types A and B usually follow a simple fall.
  • Bimalleolar and trimalleolar fractures are not bimodal — they show a type E distribution with a single peak in elderly women.
  • Kannus: a 319% increase in low-energy ankle fractures in the elderly over 1970–2000, with a predicted tripling by 2030.
Bone qualityAnkle fractures are not associated with systemic low BMD per se.
  • But the trabecular microarchitecture of the distal tibia is abnormal and depleted in elderly patients with ankle fractures.
  • Bone stiffness is reduced compared with uninjured controls.
  • This suggests these are true osteoporotic fractures.
Risk factors for sustaining an ankle fracture
  • Obesity — a prospective study of 9,704 women over 65 found ankle fractures commoner in the obese.
  • And in those with a history of multiple falls.
  • GLOW study of 60,393 women: obese women over 55 were significantly more likely to fracture.
  • Greater percentage weight increase since age 25 also raises risk.
  • Obesity also predisposes to more severe injury.
  • 29% of unstable ankle fractures were in obese patients, versus 4% of stable fractures.
  • Alcohol — 29% of patients in one series had consumed alcohol in the 4 hours preceding fracture.

Clinical Assessment

  • History, thorough physical examination, and radiographic imaging.
  • The patient's own account may reveal little — the event is too fast.
  • But appreciation of energy transfer matters.
  • High-energy mechanisms flag soft tissue complications, compartment syndrome, an actual pilon fracture, or associated injuries.
Examination sequence
  • Inspect for deformity, bruising, blistering, skin integrity and colour.
  • Palpate starting at the fibular head.
  • Progress sequentially down the lateral leg to the lateral malleolus.
  • Then the soft tissues anterior and posterior to it.
  • Then medially across the joint to the medial malleolus and its adjacent structures.
  • Palpate the skeleton of the foot for commonly missed injuries.
  • Metatarsal fractures, lateral talar process fractures, or Lisfranc disruption from a similar mechanism.
  • Palpate the Achilles and perform the Simmonds/Thompson test.
  • Distal neurovascular assessment including temperature and capillary refill. Skin-mark the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses at presentation — invaluable if the limb later deteriorates.
Never missMaisonneuve. Palpate the proximal fibula in every rotational ankle injury.
  • Highly unstable despite potentially normal ankle radiographs.
  • Where there is tenderness, obtain full-length tibia and fibula views.
Ottawa ankle rules & comorbidities that change the plan

Ottawa ankle rules (Table 65-1)

Pain near one or both malleoli plus one or more of:

  • Age over 55 years
  • Inability to bear weight
  • Bone tenderness over the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus

Highly sensitive and cost-effective, though some authors report difficulty disseminating the rules and their applicability in diabetics has been questioned.

Comorbidities that change the plan

  • Diabetes — needs preoperative work-up and perioperative glucose management.
  • Increased wound complications from immunologic and vascular impairment.
  • Poor glycaemic control predicts peripheral neuropathy.
  • That in turn influences weight-bearing decisions.
  • Smoking, alcohol abuse, psychiatric illness — all increase complication rates.

Imaging

  • Three standard views: AP, lateral and mortise.
  • The mortise view is taken in 15° internal rotation.
  • It is extremely helpful for the lateral side, poorly seen on the AP.
  • That is because of the frustral talar shape and consequent tibia/fibula/talus overlap.
  • Proximal fibular tenderness demands a full-length leg radiograph.
Reading sequence — ABCS
  • Adequacy and alignment — does this film let you make a complete assessment?
  • Bones — cortical outline and trabecular morphology, especially articular margins.
  • Cartilage — irregularity inferred from the articular surface.
  • Soft tissue contour.

Interpret with caution
  • The medial clear space more than doubles depending on rotational position of the limb.
  • It also increases significantly with plantarflexion — a common trap.
  • Absolute measurements are affected by magnification and axial rotation.
  • CT studies show many assumptions based on two-dimensional radiographs are simply not accurate.
When to get axial imaging
  • Neither CT nor MRI is used routinely.
  • CT — joint displacement in pilon fractures.
  • CT — size and configuration of a posterior malleolar fragment.
  • CT — accuracy of syndesmotic reduction postoperatively.
  • A 20% width threshold on the plain lateral reliably identifies fractures over 25% on CT, while cutting scan numbers.
  • MRI — used experimentally for deep deltoid integrity, but not common practice.
  • MRI assesses syndesmotic injury more accurately, correlating well with arthroscopy.
  • MR arthrography may add further accuracy — but both are expensive and uncommonly performed.
  • Incidental osteochondral lesions are not infrequent; their importance remains uncertain.

The ankle has three static stabilisers — the medial and lateral osteoligamentous complexes and the syndesmosis. A useful simplification: two of the three must be intact for the ankle to be stable.

The Mortise

Bony architecture of the mortise and talus
  • The talar body articulates with a confluent tibial surface: the plafond superiorly and the medial malleolus medially, enlarged posteriorly by the posterior malleolus. Laterally the talus articulates with the distal fibula.
  • The fibula carries one-sixth of the load during weight bearing.
  • The medial malleolus is both shorter and more anterior, placing the joint axis in 15° of external rotation.
  • The talus is 70% covered in articular cartilage, has no direct ligamentous attachments for muscle action, and has a tenuous retrograde blood supply.
  • The talar body is a frustum — a cone with its apex removed, lying transversely, broader anteriorly and narrower posteriorly.
Why congruence mattersRamsey and Hamilton: a 42% decrease in contact area after just 1 mm of lateral talar displacement.
  • The study has been criticised.
  • The precise relationship between displacement, contact area and pressure remains contentious.
  • But loss of mortise congruence is widely accepted as the principal cause of poor outcomes with residual talar displacement.
  • In dorsiflexion (heel strike) the talus is compressed within the mortise — the most stable position.
  • This forces the fibula to rotate externally.
  • In plantarflexion (toe-off) the talus is held less rigidly.
  • That allows physiologic external rotation and inversion.
  • Osseous stability increases with axial loading.
  • Articular congruency provides substantial stability even after division of all ligamentous restraints.

The Syndesmosis

Syndesmotic ligament anatomy and eponymous tubercles
  • The fibula sits in the incisura on the lateral tibia, stabilised by the AITFL, PITFL and the interosseous ligament (IOL), which is confluent with the interosseous membrane above.
  • The AITFL arises from the tubercle of Chaput on the anterolateral tibia (avulsed typically in paediatric injuries) and inserts on the tubercle of Wagstaffe on the fibula.
  • The PITFL arises from the Volkmann tubercle of the posterior malleolus. It is extremely strong: in trimalleolar fractures the fragment usually stays solidly attached to the fibula via this ligament.
Exploit the PITFL
  • Reduction of the distal fibula usually assists reduction of the posterior malleolus.
  • Conversely, stabilising the posterior malleolus often restores stability to the mortise.
  • It does so by reconstituting the incisura.

Medial and Lateral Ligamentous Complexes

Deltoid and lateral complex — component detail
  • Superficial deltoid — from the anterior colliculus, fanning broadly to insert into the talus, navicular and sustentaculum. This insertion is continuous with the tibialis posterior and FHL tendon sheaths.
  • Deep deltoid — intra-articular, from the posterior colliculus and intercollicular groove to the talar dome. Widely considered the key restraint against lateral talar displacement and rotation.
  • Lateral complex — the ATFL is the weakest and is commonly injured in sprains; the PTFL runs backwards from the fibular tip; the fibulocalcaneal ligament passes vertically between them to the lateral calcaneus.

Dynamic Stability

  • Static stabilisers are well characterised in cadavers.
  • But they have uncertain relevance clinically.
  • Axial loading fundamentally changes ankle behaviour.
  • It increases bony congruity restraint and stiffens the joint.
The six musculotendinous units and Michelson's finding
  • Four musculotendinous units sit at the four "corners" and act in concert.
  • Tibialis anterior — dorsiflex, invert.
  • Peroneus tertius — dorsiflex, evert.
  • Tibialis posterior — plantarflex, invert.
  • Peroneus longus and brevis — plantarflex, evert.
  • EDL and EHL augment dorsiflexion.
  • Triceps surae, plantaris, FHL and FDL augment plantarflexion.
  • Michelson: defunction both medial and lateral osteoligamentous complexes completely.
  • Talar range of movement through the gait cycle is no more than one degree in excess of intact ankles, in any plane.
ImplicationThe large volume of static biomechanical data in the literature should be viewed with extreme caution.

Surgical Anatomy — Structures Crossing the Ankle

Anterior, lateral and medial structures crossing the ankle

Anterior

  • Structures pass under the superior extensor retinaculum proximally.
  • And the Y-shaped inferior retinaculum distally.
  • Tibialis anterior is most medial, with EHL adjacent.
  • The safe plane for an anterior approach lies between them.
  • Lateral to EHL: the deep peroneal nerve and dorsalis pedis artery.
  • Then EDL and peroneus tertius.
  • Tendons and the superficial peroneal nerve are often visible and palpable with ankle and toes maximally dorsiflexed.

Lateral

  • Peronei lie deep to the peroneal retinaculum immediately posterior to the fibula; retinacular rupture causes tendon subluxation.
  • The superficial peroneal nerve emerges from deep fascia at a variable point in the distal third of the leg.
  • A substantial branch lies within 5 mm of the fibula in 50% of cases at 10 cm from the malleolar tip.
  • And in 20% at 5 cm from the tip — vulnerable in the lateral approach.
  • The sural nerve lies subcutaneously in a variable position.
  • Approximately two-thirds of the way between the distal fibula and the Achilles tendon.

Medial

  • Structures run posterior to the medial malleolus under the laciniate ligament, forming the tarsal tunnel.
  • Their anterior-to-posterior order is constant — Tom, Dick and very nervous Harry.
  • Tibialis posterior · flexor Digitorum longus · posterior tibial Artery and Vein · tibial Nerve · flexor Hallucis longus.
  • Superficially, the great saphenous vein and nerve pass immediately anterior to the medial malleolus.
  • Conveniently exposed for emergency vascular access — or inconveniently damaged during the medial approach.

Ankle fractures may be classified by anatomy, injury mechanics or stability. Multiple systems exist; only a few remain in frequent use — and none reliably predicts the presence or absence of syndesmotic rupture.

Eponyms

Volkmann, Maisonneuve, Chaput, Wagstaffe
  • Volkmann fracture — a fracture of the posterior malleolus, although first described by Earle, the grandson of Sir Percival Pott.
  • Maisonneuve fracture — proximal fibular fracture with a medial malleolar fracture or deltoid injury.
  • 5% of all ankle fractures.
  • Maisonneuve actually described fractures of both the proximal and distal fibula with rotational injury.
  • Tubercle of Chaput — anterolateral tibia, AITFL origin. Tubercle of Wagstaffe — the fibular insertion. Volkmann tubercle — posterior malleolus, PITFL origin.
Where does an ankle fracture end and a pilon begin?Uncertain, and probably moot.
  • Haraguchi — a fracture anterior to the transmalleolar line is a pilon.
  • Bartoníček — posterior collicular involvement = ankle fracture.
  • Additional anterior collicular involvement = partial pilon, excluded from his classification.
  • Mason contradicted both — types 2A/2B are "rotational pilons," type 3 a "true posterior pilon."

The literature is replete with small heterogeneous series using disparate outcome measures. Critical review suggests satisfactory outcomes follow a variety of treatments — but equally that indiscriminate surgery does not improve outcomes and exposes patients to complications.

Isolated Lateral Malleolar Fractures

Long-term evidence and immobilisation options
  • Truly isolated lateral malleolar fractures are stable, do not cause tibiotalar incongruence, and can be treated conservatively. Cadaveric work shows an isolated distal fibular fracture does not produce abnormal ankle kinematics.
  • Bauer: 98% of SER 2 fractures show no osteoarthritis at 30 years. Kristensen and Hanson: 95% good function at 21 years.
  • Surgery may increase pain and worsen function; an RCT confirmed no benefit and unnecessary complications.
  • Below-knee weight-bearing cast, elastic bandaging, air stirrup, ankle brace, or a stabilising shoe are all satisfactory — no differences beyond 3 months. For stable Weber B, 3 weeks of immobilisation is non-inferior to 6.
  • Ongoing symptoms may reflect cartilage damage at injury: Hintermann found chondral damage to the talus in 60% and to the fibula in 50% of isolated AO type B fractures.
Why the SER 2 "displacement" is illusoryMichelson and Harper, on CT:
  • The distal fibular fragment remains anatomically wedded to the talus.
  • The talus in turn keeps its normal relationship to the plafond.
  • The apparent malalignment is internal rotation of the proximal fragment.
  • Not external rotation of the distal one.
  • Several millimetres of apparent displacement on plain films is less than 1 mm on CT.

The Occult Instability Problem

  • The central diagnostic dilemma in ankle trauma.
  • Stable isolated lateral malleolar fracture (SER 2)?
  • Or a lateral malleolar fracture with a deltoid rupture (SER 4)?
  • The problem arises when the mortise is enlocated on presentation radiographs.
Stress views — why the evidence collapsed
  • Manual external rotation stress and the gravity stress view give equivalent results.
  • Gravity view: lateral decubitus, injured side down, foot hanging free.
  • It reduces surgeon radiation and patient discomfort.
  • Avoid plantarflexion — it produces apparent but not true displacement.
  • There is no agreed cut-off. Absolute medial clear space of 4 or 5 mm is often quoted, but patient size, gender, magnification and rotation all affect it.
  • Egol: of 101 apparently stable isolated lateral malleolar fractures, 66 had a "positive" test at >4 mm. The 30 with a positive test but no clinical signs had identical results at 7–10 months whether treated operatively or functionally, and none developed instability.
  • Tornetta: 114 patients with a positive test at 5 mm; 54 treated non-operatively united with an anatomical mortise.
  • Another cohort of 155: at 4 mm, 79% positive — all treated successfully non-operatively, hence false positives. Raising the cut-off to 6 mm still left 19% false positive; requiring >6 mm and greater than baseline still left 15%.
  • Koval: 21 patients with 5–8 mm clear space all had superficial deltoid rupture on MRI, but only 2 had complete deep deltoid rupture. The other 19 had partial deep tears, were treated conservatively with full weight bearing, and all recovered fully.
  • COTS RCT: isolated lateral malleolar fracture with 5 mm medial clear space randomised to surgery or not — no difference in function at 1 year. Six operated patients had wound complications requiring further treatment and worse outcomes.
  • Judged against stress views, clinical signs fare little better.
  • Tenderness — positive predictive value under 50%.
  • Absent tenderness — negative predictive value 66%.
  • Absent tenderness and bruising — negative predictive value 39%.
  • But the stress test is itself a poor gold standard.
  • So the low negative predictive value of clinical signs may have been overstated.
  • Absence of medial tenderness, swelling or bruising probably is a good indicator of functional stability.
Pragmatic answer — the walking testThe stress examination is "truly insufficient in determining ankle instability."
  • Removable boot orthosis in the emergency department.
  • Allow full weight bearing.
  • Review at one week with radiographs.
  • Mortise displaced → offer surgery.
  • No displacement despite weight bearing → the ankle is stable.
  • Review at 6 weeks for final assessment.

Medial Malleolar Fractures

Classification and the case for non-operative treatment
  • Herscovici's classification by fracture level is the most-cited. Pankovitch described anterior collicular fractures ± deep deltoid rupture, posterior collicular fractures, supracollicular fractures (vertical, oblique, transverse) and avulsion chips.
  • Muller recommended screw fixation of all medial malleolar fractures in 1945, but isolated fractures can generally be managed non-operatively: Herscovici's 57 patients, many displaced up to 6 mm, had only 2 nonunions (3.5%), no mortise displacement and no osteoarthritis at 3 years.
  • Hanhisuanto: displacement ≤2 mm can be treated non-operatively with comparable outcomes. Regardless of treatment, greater initial displacement predicted poorer function.
  • In bi/trimalleolar injuries, if the medial malleolus reduces to ≤2 mm after fibular stabilisation, emerging evidence supports non-operative management of the medial side.
Why fixing the medial side alone failsYablon: "the displacement of the talus faithfully follows that of the lateral malleolus."
  • Tornetta stressed ankles after medial but before lateral fixation.
  • A quarter remained unstable.
  • Medial fixation alone reestablishes stability in only three-quarters of bimalleolar fractures.
  • Pankovich explained why: fixation restores the superficial deltoid.
  • But the deep deltoid, attached to the posterior colliculus, has already ruptured and stays incompetent.

Posterior Malleolus

  • Around one-third of ankle fractures involve the posterior malleolus.
  • Outcomes are worse when they do.
  • Likely from cartilage damage at the time of injury.
  • Or from talar instability, posterior subluxation and incongruity.
  • Almost everything else about it remains controversial.
The biomechanical surprise — how little the posterior malleolus contributes
  • The posterior malleolus contributes very little to joint contact, load bearing or stability.
  • A 25% fragment causes no significant decrease in contact area; 33% reduces it by only 15%; 50% by around 30%.
  • In an unconstrained loaded cadaveric model with a 50% fracture, contact stresses were not increased — they were redistributed anteriorly and laterally, away from the fracture line and the supposedly vulnerable cartilage edge.
  • Resections of up to 50% of the plafond are stable in the presence of intact or fixed medial or lateral structures.
Does fixing it help? The evidence

Potential benefits: reconstructing the plafond, removing intercalated fragments, stabilising the talus, and reconstructing the incisura to facilitate fibular reduction and syndesmotic stability.

  • No systematic review has confirmed benefit from surgery except when it corrects talar subluxation.
  • Harper: 38 patients with fragments over 25% — reduction, union, complications and late OA were the same operatively and non-operatively. De Vries at 9–30 years: no difference in pain, function or arthritis. Langenhuijsen: neither size nor fixation influenced outcome.
  • Mertens and Myers both found operatively treated fragments fared worse.
  • Hoogendoorn RCT (Weber B, fragments 5–25%): difference in final step-off between groups was 0.5 mm, with no difference in function, osteoarthritis or complications at 1 year.
  • Franx RCT (26 Weber C fractures): the fixed group had better functional scores at 1 year.
  • Complications of fixation run 17–20%: infection, sural neurapraxia, loss of reduction, arthritis.
  • More than 2 mm final step-off gives worse outcomes regardless of treatment — but do not assume fixation reduces better: in some large series the final congruency of fixed fragments was no better, or worse, than non-operatively treated fragments.

The syndesmotic argument

  • The PITFL is usually intact after a posterior malleolar fracture.
  • So reduction and stabilisation reconstitutes the incisura.
  • That in turn stabilises the syndesmosis.
  • Gardner: a PER 4 fracture was more stable after posterior malleolar fixation than after a syndesmosis screw.
  • Confirmed clinically by others across a variety of fracture patterns.

Syndesmotic Injury

Boden, fibular fracture level, and when fixation is unnecessary
  • Diastasis requires rupture of three strong ligaments plus the interosseous membrane — a very substantial insult, often with a poor long-term outcome with or without surgery.
  • Pettrone demonstrated poorer outcomes at 5 years with radiographic widening; diastasis must be identified at the outset and stabilised.
  • Boden: where the medial-sided injury is a malleolar fracture, fracture fixation renders the syndesmosis stable — confirmed by further cadaveric and clinical studies. The exception may be a fixed anterior collicular fracture with a ruptured deep deltoid.
  • Boden also showed that PER fractures created more than 3–4.5 cm proximal to the ankle required syndesmotic fixation — but that assumed the membrane was torn up to the fracture level, and MRI has shown this relationship is not constant. Even with a deltoid tear, fractures up to 5 cm proximal can be treated non-operatively with equally satisfactory results at 1 year.
  • The proximal fibular fracture itself does not require fixation — exploration there risks the common peroneal nerve.
Intraoperative testing and the malreduction problem
  • Options: lateral force on the heel, direct lateral pull on the fibula with a hook (hook test) or clamp, or external rotation stress. The tests correlate poorly with one another and have limited sensitivity against a standardised 7.5 Nm external rotation test.
  • Stoffel found the hook test most useful; external rotation force consistently increased only the medial clear space — and that also happened with deltoid sectioning even with an intact syndesmosis.
  • Beumer found all clinical tests produced radiographic "abnormalities" that could occur without any anatomic injury.
  • The hook test is the first choice of 64% of surgeons.
  • Malreduction: ~50% after closed reduction, ~15% after open. Fluoroscopy cannot detect up to 30° of fibular rotation within the syndesmosis, so malreduction often requires postoperative CT to recognise.
  • Sagi reported significantly worse function with malreduction, though criticised for not specifying the degree and scanning 2 years post-injury. Andersen found a ≥2 mm side-to-side difference in anterior tibiofibular distance predicted poorer outcome; other authors found no functional impairment with malreduced syndesmoses.
  • Kennedy randomised PER fractures with and without syndesmosis screws and showed a worse radiographic outcome among those screwed. There is clinical evidence that syndesmotic fixation in unstable SER 4 fractures does not improve outcome.

Aftercare

Weight bearing, movement and return to driving
  • Weight bearing in plaster after fixation is safe with no increase in major complications — identified by the Cochrane database as one of the few well-investigated areas of surgical treatment. Weight bearing in an orthosis, allowing early active movement, also appears safe.
  • A systematic review of 10 RCTs and 4 cohorts found early weight bearing gave superior function at 6 weeks with no increase in complications. Despite this, surgeons remain conservative with high rates of restricted weight bearing.
  • Exclusions from early weight bearing: dementia, neuropathy, IV drug use, excessive alcohol, and potentially unstable fixation.
  • Early active movement is safe but shows no long-term benefit over immobilisation. No convincing benefit has been shown for manual therapy, passive stretching or specific training programmes.
  • Driving — braking reaction time is significantly increased in a cast or brace, even in healthy volunteers. Impairment persists after cast removal but resolves by 9 weeks post-op, so patients can usually resume around 3 weeks after cast removal. The authors' pragmatic guide: the ability to descend stairs reciprocally, one foot per step.

Conservative treatment of unstable fractures — the historical case
  • Wei — 19 conservatively managed unstable bi/trimalleolar fractures at a mean of 20 years.
  • Above-knee cast 6 weeks, then below-knee 6 weeks, with close surveillance.
  • Only two patients were "mildly symptomatic"; mean AOFAS 98.
  • Joy — 118 unstable fractures.
  • Anatomical reduction of the talus under the plafond gave a good result in 85%.
  • Poor reduction gave a poor result in two-thirds.
  • Willett multicentre RCT — patients over 65, fixation versus close-contact casting under GA.
  • No difference in patient-reported outcome at 6 months.
  • But 10% wound problems in the surgical group.
  • And 25% of non-surgical patients failed — remanipulation or conversion to ORIF.
  • A further 15% went on to malunion, with poorer function at 3 years.
  • Principle: with accurate reduction and vigilant monitoring, non-operative treatment can give a good result.
  • Drawback: maintaining reduction is difficult.
  • And a prolonged non-weight-bearing casting regimen is now considered burdensome.

Four approaches cover essentially all malleolar surgery. The deep plane for the posteromedial and posterolateral approaches is identical — the interval between flexor hallucis longus and the peroneal tendons. Only the skin incision differs.

Decide position before you scrub
  • Fixed supine, the fibular plate or nail obscures fluoroscopic assessment of the posterior malleolus.
  • If a posterior plate is then needed, turning from supine to prone is fraught with delay and hazard.
  • There is no certain way to identify the rare unstable posterior injury.
  • So judge in advance. Position prone from the outset if the patient is young and active.
  • Or if there is a large fragment, marginal impaction, or clear syndesmotic injury.

Post-traumatic Osteoarthritis

Risk factors, cartilage damage and time course
  • Ankle osteoarthritis is most commonly post-traumatic, with 39% of cases secondary to ankle fracture.
  • Risk factors: OTA/AO type C patterns, high BMI, dislocation, increased age.
  • Arthritis may occur even after perfect reduction, presumably from cartilage damage at the time of injury — assessing who is at risk is a major challenge.
  • Stufkens: cartilage damage predicted OA at a mean of almost 13 years. No correlation with the number of lesions, but worse outcomes with deeper lesions and those on the anterior or lateral talus or the medial malleolus.
  • Arthroscopic assessment shows the commonest site of damage is the talus, then the distal tibia, then the fibula, then the medial malleolus.
  • Horisberger: mean time from ankle fracture to end-stage osteoarthritis is 21 years.

Predictors of Poorer Outcome

Predictors of poorer outcome
  • Presenting with a fracture dislocation.
  • Fracture involving the medial malleolus rather than a medial ligamentous injury.
  • Older, female and diabetic patients.
  • Postoperative complications correlate both with worse patient-reported outcomes and with the development of osteoarthritis.

Return to Sport

  • Sport is the third most common cause of ankle fractures.
  • Results vary widely with cohort demographics.
Return-to-sport series
  • Porter: 27 operatively treated athletes (70% male, mean age 18) — all but one returned to their previous level at a mean of 2.4 years.
  • Shah: only 62% returned to their prior level at 5 years.
  • Colvin: patients who had participated in sport before injury (55% male, mean age 43) — only 24.5% had returned to full sporting activity at 1 year.
Metalwork removalRemoval improves patient-reported outcomes in only 50% of patients.
  • 65% of surgeons remove syndesmosis screws routinely.
  • Many studies show no difference in movement or function between intact, removed and broken screws.
  • Some report better outcomes with fractured or loose screws.
  • Schepers after removal: 9.2% infection, 6.6% recurrent diastasis, 22.4% overall complications.
  • On-demand removal was non-inferior to routine removal, with fewer complications.
